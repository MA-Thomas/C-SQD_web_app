Do people respond similarly to hypothetical moral dilemmas as they do to real-life 
dilemma situations? Bostyn et al. (2018) explored this question by confronting one group 
of participants with a real dilemma involving mice, and another with a hypothetical 
description of that dilemma situation. In the current manuscript, we report the results of 
three new data collections involving the same research paradigm aimed at replicating 
and extending this earlier work. One sample included a within-subjects condition where 
participants encountered both versions of the dilemma which demonstrated that a 
significant proportion of participants (19%) change their decisions when moving from the 
hypothetical to real-life context. Switching behavior showed an asymmetry: participants 
were more likely to switch from rejecting to accepting sacrificial harm than vice versa. 
However, in contrast to earlier findings, we find that responses to hypothetical dilemma 
batteries significantly predict real-life dilemma decisions. This work reinforces the utility 
of using hypothetical scenarios to study moral psychology while also demonstrating that 
responses can differ systematically between hypothetical and real-life contexts. 
Introduction  
Psychologists study moral cognition by probing people 
with various types of moral dilemmas. As confronting par-
ticipants with real-life dilemma situations is fraught with 
ethical as well as practical concerns, most researchers opt 
to have participants respond to text-based, hypothetical 
dilemmas instead. This approach has proven to be valuable 
in gaining insight into moral cognition (see for instance, 
Awad et al., 2020; Cushman & Greene, 2012; Greene, 2014). 
However, while decades of research have established that 
attitudes generally predict behavior (Ajzen, 2001; Kraus, 
1995), moral dilemmas may represent a special case. Real-
life moral decisions differ fundamentally from hypothetical 
ones: They evoke stronger emotions, have actual conse-
quences, and people seem to make different choices when 
facing more realistic versions of moral dilemmas (Francis 
et al., 2016, 2017; Patil et al., 2014). Bostyn et al. (2018) 
explored this issue by directly comparing how people re-
spond to a hypothetical or a real-life version of the same 
moral dilemma and uncovered differences as well as simi-
larities. The current manuscript reports the findings from 
new data collections (total n = 727) using the same para-
digm and provides an updated perspective on how hypo-
thetical and real-life moral decisions relate. 
On Sacrificial Dilemmas    
By and large, the most studied moral dilemmas are “sac-
rificial harm” dilemmas (Greene et al., 2001, 2004). These 
dilemmas ask one to ponder whether it is morally good to 
actively inflict a smaller amount of harm if doing so ensures 
that greater harm is averted. Consider the paradigmatic ex-
ample: A runaway trolley train is about to hit five unsus-
pecting workmen laboring on the track. The only way to 
avert this disastrous outcome is to pull a switch and divert 
the trolley to a second track where it will run over a single 
workman instead. Would it be morally appropriate to sacri-
fice the single workman if doing so would save the lives of 
the group of five?

search because they are assumed to contrast two conflicting 
ethical frameworks. Utilitarianism suggests that the 
morally appropriate course of action can be determined by

Realism and Dilemmas    
Research using hypothetical dilemmas has demon-
strated that people’s judgments on these types of dilemmas 
can be fickle. While people generally give similar responses 
across dilemmas of a dilemma battery, small changes to the 
structure within a dilemma can result in a dramatic shift 
in the type of judgment that people favor (Cushman et al., 
2006; Greene et al., 2009; Hauser et al., 2007). Accordingly, 
there are multiple reasons why one might expect hypothet-
ical and real-life moral judgment to differ. 
First, reading about a dilemma, no matter how vividly it 
is described, is a psychologically distinct experience from 
encountering a dilemma in real life. Research has demon-
strated that emotionality impacts moral judgment (Gle-
ichgerrcht & Young, 2013; Tasso et al., 2017), and respond-
ing to a real-life dilemma is a more emotionally impactful 
experience. Moreover, hypothetical dilemmas are, by their 
very nature, hypothetical. Decisions made on hypothetical 
dilemmas are inconsequential, whereas decisions made on 
real-life dilemmas can have far-reaching impacts. Finally, 
research that has attempted to make hypothetical dilemma 
research more realistic has found that such manipulations 
impact people’s moral decisions. For instance, when people 
are confronted with Virtual Reality versions of dilemmas, 
they are more likely to favor “utilitarian” responses (Francis 
et al., 2016, 2017; Patil et al., 2014). 
The Mice Dilemma    
Bostyn et al. (2018) conducted a study that confronted 
participants with either a hypothetical or a real-life version 
of the same dilemma. For the real-life case, they invited 
participants on an individual basis to the lab where they 
confronted them with an electroshock machine that was 
hooked up to two cages of mice. Crucially, one cage con-
tained five mice, whereas the other contained only a single 
mouse. They told participants that five mice would receive 
a painful shock once a countdown timer of 20 seconds 
reached zero. They also told them that if they so desired, 
they could press a button that would lead to the single 
mouse receiving a similarly painful shock instead. No ac-
tual electroshocks were delivered to the animals, as this 
set-up involved misdirection: Once the timer reached zero 
or participants had pressed the button, the experiment was 
halted, and participants were debriefed. A second group 
of participants completed a text-based version of the mice 
dilemma. 
Bostyn et al. (2018) found that participants were more 
likely to favor sacrificial harm when confronted with the 
real-life mice dilemma in comparison to the text-based ver-
sion. Moreover, they found that responses to a battery of 
(hypothetical) traditional text-based dilemmas predicted 
how participants responded to the hypothetical mice 
dilemma but did not seem to predict participants’ decisions 
on the real-life mice dilemma. Responses to hypothetical 
dilemmas did predict how uncomfortable and uncertain 
participants felt about their real-life decisions, as well has 
how quickly they reacted, suggesting that hypothetical 
preferences still captured meaningful aspects of moral de-
cision-making, even if they did not directly predict behav-
ior. Nevertheless, based on these initial results, they con-
cluded that important differences between hypothetical 
and real-life moral judgment exist. 
Commentaries and Debate    
These findings instigated a scholarly debate on their 
interpretation and implications. The original manuscript 
drew four separate commentaries (Białek et al., 2019; Col-
man et al., 2019; Evans & Brandt, 2019; Plunkett & Greene, 
2019) and a subsequent reply (Bostyn & Roets, 2019). Bi-
ałek et al. (2019) argued that while responses to hypothet-
ical dilemmas might not perfectly predict behavior, this 
does not invalidate their utility. They emphasized that most 
people rarely encounter real-life sacrificial dilemmas but do 
judge others who face such decisions. They suggested that 
understanding how people think about moral dilemmas re-
mains valuable even if responses to hypothetical dilem-
mas do not predict behavior. In their reply, Bostyn & Roets 
(2019) acknowledged this point, agreeing that hypotheti-
cal dilemma research remains valuable for understanding 
how people evaluate others’ moral character. However, they 
also emphasized that understanding potential differences 
between hypothetical judgment and real behavior is impor-
tant for advancing moral psychology. 
Colman et al. (2019) questioned the use of deception in 
the experimental design and argued that participant skep-
ticism about the use of real shocks could have compromised 
the results. They advocated for avoiding deception in psy-
chological research, citing evidence that deceptive practices 
can affect both current and future studies by making partic-
ipants more suspicious of experimental procedures. In re-
sponse, Bostyn & Roets (2019) suggested that proper de-
briefing can mitigate potential negative effects and noted 
that controlling for participant skepticism did not alter any 
results in their analyses. 
Evans and Brandt (2019) focused on statistical method-
ology. They noted that Bostyn et al. (2018) examined hy-
pothetical and real-life groups separately, finding that the 
responses to hypothetical dilemma batteries predicted de-
cisions in the hypothetical condition but not in the real-
life condition. However, Evans and Brandt correctly pointed 
out that finding one relationship significant and another

difference scores (combining utilitarian and deontological

combined scores predicted real-life behavior. More broadly,

predicting individual differences in behavior. In their reply,

proposed approach. They noted that according to the dual-
process model, deontological and utilitarian processes are

2013; Greene et al., 2004). Moreover, they argued that dif-

treating participants who score high on both preferences 
the same as those who score low on both. They also pointed 
out that including a difference score is mathematically su-
perfluous compared to a linear model that already contains 
both variables as separate predictors. 
The Present Studies    
Despite this debate, further empirical studies have been 
notably absent. The present manuscript aims to extend our 
understanding of how hypothetical and real-life moral de-
cisions relate. Using the same sacrificial dilemma as in 
Bostyn et al. (2018), i.e., the mice dilemma, we gathered 
three new samples. We also tried to improve on the original 
methodology in several ways. Most importantly, these new 
samples incorporated an expanded set of moral preference 
measures to test the robustness of hypothetical prefer-
ences’ predictive power for real-life decisions. Additionally, 
we included a within-subjects sample in which some partic-
ipants were exposed to both the hypothetical and the real-
life dilemma, allowing us to examine whether individuals 
can accurately predict their own behavior when encounter-
ing the same scenario in both contexts. By combining the 
newly gathered data with that from Bostyn et al. (2018), 
we aim to address two key questions: 1) Do people respond 
differently to a text-based hypothetical dilemma than they 
do when facing the exact same dilemma in real life, and 
2) Do responses on hypothetical dilemma batteries predict 
what people do when confronted with a real-life sacrificial 
dilemma?

Measures  
Sacrificial Dilemma Battery    
To measure participants’ preference for “utilitarian” and 
“deontological” moral judgment, they were asked to com-
plete a battery of ten hypothetical sacrificial dilemmas pre-
viously used in Bostyn et al. (2018). Each dilemma de-
scribed a situation in which harm to a larger group of 
people could be avoided by harming a single person (or a 
smaller group of people). On each dilemma, participants 
indicated both to what extent they considered the util-
itarian option to be morally appropriate, as well as the 
extent to which they considered the deontological option 
to be morally appropriate, using a 5-point scale ranging 
from (1) Absolutely inappropriate to (5) Absolutely appro-
priate. We constructed overall scores of participants’ ap-
proval for “utilitarian” and “deontological” judgment by 
averaging their responses across all ten dilemmas. An ex-

Process Dissociation Task    
The Process Dissociation Task provided an alternate 
measure for participants’ preference for “utilitarian” and 
“deontological” judgment. This approach addresses a key 
limitation of traditional binary moral judgments, which 
conflate utilitarian and deontological preferences into a 
single response. Conway and Gawronski (2013) developed 
this method to separately estimate people’s utilitarian (U-
parameter) and deontological (D-parameter) inclinations 
by comparing responses to two types of dilemmas. 
Incongruent dilemmas are similar to traditional sacrifi-
cial dilemmas where utilitarian and deontological concerns 
conflict (e.g., torturing one person to stop a bomb from ex-
ploding and saving many lives). Congruent dilemmas in-
volve the same general scenarios but the benefit obtained 
from sacrificial harm has been reduced (e.g., torturing one 
person to stop a paint bomb from exploding and vandaliz-
ing a bus stop). In congruent dilemmas, both utilitarian and 
deontological reasoning oppose the harmful action because 
the benefits are insufficient to justify the harm. 
Participants received a set of twenty dilemmas, half of 
which were congruent and half of which were incongruent, 
and they were asked to judge in a binary way whether the 
sacrificial harm was morally appropriate or inappropriate. 
Process Dissociation leverages the contrast between these 
dilemma types to isolate the strength of each psychologi-
cal process. If someone has strong utilitarian inclinations, 
they should approve of harm more often in incongruent 
dilemmas (where it serves a greater good) than in con-
gruent dilemmas (where benefits are trivial). If someone 
has strong deontological inclinations, they should consis-
tently reject harm across both dilemma types. By compar-
ing participants’ approval rates across these dilemma types, 
multinomial modelling can mathematically estimate inde-
pendent measures of their utilitarian (U-parameter) and 
deontological (D-parameter) inclinations. A processing tree 
demonstrating this logic, as well as a more thorough ex-
planation of this procedure, is available through Conway & 
Gawronski (2013). Research suggests this approach yields 
better predictions than simple binary judgments because it 
disentangles typically conflated processes (Conway et al., 
2018; Conway & Gawronski, 2013). 
Oxford Utilitarianism Scale    
Participants completed the 9-item Oxford Utilitarianism 
Scale (Kahane et al., 2018) to obtain a non-dilemma-based 
measure of their moral preferences. This scale measures 
participants’ approval of two aspects of utilitarian judg-
ment: “Instrumental Harm” and “Impartial Beneficence”. 
Participants were asked to indicate their agreement using 
a 7-point scale ranging from (1) Completely disagree to (7) 
Completely agree. Subscale scores were constructed by av-
eraging the responses to items of that subscale. An exam-
ple item for the Instrumental Harm subscale reads: “It is 
morally right to harm an innocent person if harming them 
is a necessary means to helping several other innocent peo-
ple.” An example item for the Impartial Beneficence sub-
scale reads: “From a moral perspective, people should care 
about the well-being of all human beings on the planet 
equally; they should not favor the well-being of people who 
are especially close to them either physically or emotion-
ally.” 
Real-life Mice Dilemma    
In both the real-life and the within-subject samples, par-
ticipants were invited to the lab on an individual basis. 
All participants were asked to wear a mobile electrocar-
diogram monitor during the real-life dilemma administra-
tion. These physiological measurements were included to 
investigate whether heart rate variability (HRV) is related 
to the choices people make in the real-life dilemma. HRV is 
very sensitive to physical movement. Participants were in-
structed to remain still during real-life dilemma adminis-
trations. However, most of them were so surprised by the 
real-life dilemma that they ended up moving anyway (e.g. 
grasping their mouth in surprise). As a result, we do not be-
lieve that any meaningful conclusions can be drawn from 
this data and do not discuss this aspect of the methodol-
ogy in the current manuscript. The relevant methodology is 
available through the supplementary materials. 
The inclusion of the ECG measure necessitated a number 
of tweaks to the real-life dilemma methodology used in 
Bostyn et al. (2018). In Bostyn et al. (2018), participants ar-
rived individually and were guided into a lab room where 
they were immediately confronted with the real-life 
dilemma setup. In the current samples, participants waited 
in a queue in front of the lab rooms, which caused some 
participants to see others waiting for their turn. To maxi-
mize data collection efficiency, we ran sessions simultane-
ously in two lab rooms. Each room contained a basic setup: 
a chair, a table, and a laptop. While one participant encoun-
tered the moral dilemma, another participant in the second 
room completed the baseline ECG measure, allowing us to 
alternate between rooms with the real-life dilemma equip-
ment. 
When participants entered the lab, they were first seated 
at a laptop in one of the rooms. They were told that we 
needed to collect a baseline heart rate measurement before 
starting the actual experiment. The experimenter in-
structed them to sit quietly and look at a blank screen, ex-
plaining that someone would return to continue the exper-

Hypothetical Mice Dilemma    
Participants in the hypothetical dilemma sample com-
pleted the dilemma as part of an online survey. Participants 
in the within-subject sample were invited to the lab in in-
dividual sessions for the hypothetical dilemma (about one 
week before returning for the real-life dilemma). After com-
pleting the preparatory work for ECG measurement, they 
were seated in front of a computer screen. Participants first 
completed two short experiments unrelated to the current 
study: one on empathy for outgroup members and one on 
intergroup contact. After participants completed both ex-
periments, they were asked to sit still for a single minute 
and attend to a blank screen to allow their heart rate to re-
turn to baseline and minimize any potential impact from 
completing the prior two experiments. After one minute, 
a computer screen automatically displayed a hypothetical 
version of the mice dilemma described above. Once partici-
pants had read the scenario, they could continue to the next 
screen, at which point they were asked whether they would 
or would not press the button when confronted with this 
dilemma. An exact translation of the dilemma is available 
through the supplementary materials. 
Debriefing Questionnaire   
After completing the real-life mice dilemma, an experi-
menter debriefed participants and asked them several fol-
low-up questions related to how they experienced the 
dilemma. Participants were asked what decision they had 
made, and why they had made this decision (open-ended). 
These motivations were probed verbally and immediately 
paraphrased by the experiment leader. Additionally, par-
ticipants were asked to what extent they found it morally 
appropriate to divert the shock from the five mice to the 
single mouse, to what extent they found it morally appro-
priate not to divert the shock, to rate to what extent they 
doubted their decision, had deliberated on their decision, 
how comfortable they felt while making the decision, how 
guilty they felt for making that decision, and to what extent 
they felt they had broken a social norm with their decision. 
Participants were also asked whether they thought they 
would have made another choice if they had been given 
more time, to what extent they thought others would find 
their choice morally appropriate, and what they thought 
the likelihood was that no shocks would be given during 
the experiment. Finally, we asked participants what they 
had heard about this experiment to gauge whether knowl-
edge of our experimental procedure had spread among our 
participant population. This did not appear to be the case. 
Once the debriefing questionnaire was completed, we ex-
plained the purpose of the experiment and answered any 
additional questions they had. 
Results  
Do People Respond Differently to a Text-based        
Hypothetical Dilemma than when Faced with the        
Same Dilemma in Real Life?      
For the most direct and meaningful test of this question, 
we analyzed the data from the within-subject sample. In 
this sample, participants were initially confronted with the 
hypothetical dilemma and were exposed to the real-life ver-
sion one week later. The recruitment message at the on-
set of our experiment notified participants that they would 
be confronted with a real-life dilemma involving animals. 
Upon reading the hypothetical dilemma, it is thus likely 
that many participants realized they would be confronting 
that same dilemma in a later session and that they also 
remembered how they had responded to the hypothetical 
dilemma when completing the real-life one.

At an initial glance, the sample seemed to respond sim-
ilarly across dilemma modalities: an equal number of par-
ticipants, i.e., 76%, decided in favor of committing sacrifi-
cial harm (the “utilitarian” option) when confronted with 
either the hypothetical or the real-life dilemma. Moreover, 
81% of participants responded in the same way to both 
versions of the dilemma. This consistency demonstrates 
that most participants are able to accurately predict their 
own moral behavior when confronted with the same sce-
nario in both contexts. Nevertheless, 19% of participants 
did switch their responses across dilemma modalities. This 
is notable given both the relative simplicity of the dilemma 
and that participants had every reason to maintain con-
sistent responses across the clearly connected scenarios. 
Moreover, the tendency to switch appeared to be asym-
metric. Of the participants who had favored the “utilitar-
ian” decision (i.e., shocking the single mouse) when con-
fronted with the hypothetical dilemma (the largest group), 
only 12% switched to the ‘deontological’ option when con-
fronted with the real-life dilemma1
. In contrast, of the par-
ticipants who had initially favored the ‘deontological’ op-
tion, 40% switched to the ‘utilitarian’ option when 
confronted with the real-life dilemma. A formal (non-pre-
registered) test of this asymmetry showed that this differ-
ence is statistically significant χ2(1) = 14.67, p < .001: par-
ticipants were substantially more likely to switch from a 
hypothetical ‘deontological’ response to a real-life ‘utilitar-
ian’ response than the other way around. 
These findings suggest that a raw comparison of the 
overall proportion of “deontological” or “utilitarian” judg-
ments across dilemma modalities can obscure meaningful 
shifts in moral dilemma judgment. Nevertheless, Bostyn et 
al. (2018) conducted such a comparison, and accordingly, 
we also looked at how participants responded in the two 
between-subject samples we collected. Figure 2 shows the 
proportion of participants favoring the “deontological” op-
tion in Bostyn et al. (2018), and in the new hypothetical and 
real-life dilemma sample. Bostyn et al. (2018) reported that 
people who received the hypothetical dilemma were more 
likely to favor the “deontological” response compared to 
those who were confronted with the real-life version. This 
pattern is not replicated in the newly collected data, which 
showed a substantially higher percentage of deontological 
decisions compared to the 2018 data. 
Some caution is warranted when interpreting these re-
sults. First, like Bostyn et al. (2018), we allotted 20 seconds 
for participants to respond to the real-life dilemma. Inter-
estingly, 16% of the participants who were registered as 
making a “deontological” decision in the real-life dilemma 
still claimed during the debriefing that they had actually 
wanted to make a “utilitarian” decision but had acted too 
late (i.e., after the deadline). Apparently, some participants 
had hesitated, postponed making a decision until the last 
moment, and ended up acting too late. Some participants 
were aware of their tardiness, while others were not. This 
latter group, under the incorrect conviction that they had 
pressed the button in time, even ended up motivating their 
decision by claiming it was better to minimize harm. Inter-
estingly, a (non-preregistered) t-test does demonstrate that 
reaction times in the original study (M = 10.12 seconds) 
were significantly faster than those in the newly gathered 
real-life sample (M = 11.65 seconds), t(333.66) = -2.96, p 
= .003. While this is speculative, it is possible that proce-
dural changes might have caused participants to act more 
slowly. In the Bostyn et al. (2018) study, participants were 
immediately confronted with the dilemma when entering 
the lab and made decisions from a standing position. In 
the new samples, participants were seated and underwent 
a three-minute waiting period for baseline heart rate mea-
surement. Perhaps this more relaxed physical state led to 
slower response times and a corresponding increase in late 
decisions. 
Additionally, one other factor seems to have contributed 
to a higher proportion of participants favoring the “deon-
tological” decision in the new samples. As mentioned pre-
viously, participants queued up outside the lab rooms. As 
a result, some participants saw others start the experiment 
before they completed their own session. Some participants 
seem to have taken this into account when making their de-
cisions. Approximately 17% of participants making “deon-
tological” decisions claimed during the debriefing that their 
judgment was motivated by a desire to balance the spread 
of shocks between the group and the single mouse. These 
participants reasoned that most other participants would 
shock the single mouse and wanted to give that mouse 
some reprieve (for similar ‘balancing’ behavior, see Bostyn 
et al., in press). In Bostyn et al. (2018), participants did 
not queue up, and they completed the experiment with-
out meeting other participants, making this concern less 
salient. Note that such considerations likely also did not 
cross the minds of participants completing the hypothetical 
dilemma. 
As a result, it is not straightforward to directly compare 
the response tendencies for the hypothetical and real-life 
moral decisions. An uncorrected comparison of response 
rates shows no significant difference between conditions 
χ2(1) = 0.38, p = .54, but that comparison does not take into 
account the contextual factors that were unique to the real-
life setting: i.e. that some participants acted too late, and 
that others made deontological choices because they con-
sidered the choices of other participants. Removing these 
responses from the analysis does yield a significant differ-
ence between conditions, χ2(1) = 6.39, p = .011, but such a 
test is post-hoc as we did not plan for such corrections. The 
most prudent conclusion might be to consider these results 
as inconclusive.

Do Responses on Hypothetical Dilemma Batteries       
Predict What People Do When Confronted With a         
Real-life Sacrificial Dilemma?    
Bostyn et al. (2018) found that people’s responses to a 
text-based sacrificial dilemma battery did not predict how 
they responded to the real-life mice dilemma. In contrast, 
in the new real-life dilemma sample, we find that these re-
sponses do predict how participants respond to the real-
life dilemma. These associative patterns are not strong, but 
both the degree to which participants approved of the util-
itarian options in the sacrificial dilemma battery, standard-
ized OR = 1.37, z = 2.05, p = .040, and the extent to which 
they approved of the deontological options, standardized 
OR = 0.76, z = -2.00, p = .046, significantly predicted how 
participants reacted to the real-life dilemma. We also find 
a similar pattern of results in the within-subject sample, 
with effects being somewhat stronger for utilitarian ap-
proval (standardized OR = 1.77, z = 2.82, p = .005) and some-
what weaker for deontological approval standardized OR = 
0.72, z = -1.90, p = .057 (see Figure 3). 
Combining data from all three samples (Bostyn et al., 
2018, new real-life sample, and within-subject sample) into 
a mixed-model analysis (with a random intercept for Study) 
yields a significant positive relationship of utilitarian ap-
proval ratings with real-life utilitarian decisions, OR = 1.69, 
z = 3.48, p < .001 and a significant negative relationship 
for deontological approval ratings, OR = 0.59, z = -3.18, p = 
.001. These meta-analytic results suggest modest but reli-
able relationships between hypothetical moral preferences 
and real-life decisions across studies. 
We also find a similar pattern of results for the other 
moral preference measures included in the new samples. 
The U-parameter estimated through the process dissoci-
ation approach in the additional dilemma battery (which 
corresponds with a measure for participants’ tendencies to-
wards utilitarian judgment) predicted real-life dilemma re-
sponses, both in the real-life dilemma sample, standardized 
OR = 1.47, z = 2.49, p = .013, and the within-subject sample, 
standardized OR = 1.73, z = 2.86, p = .004. The D-parameter 
(which corresponds with the tendency towards deontologi-
cal judgment) did not significantly predict real-life dilemma 
decisions; not in the real-life dilemma sample, standard-
ized OR = 0.76, z = -1.77, p = .077, nor in the within-subject 
sample, standardized OR = 0.68, z = -1.69, p = .091. 
The Instrumental Harm subscale of the Oxford Utilitar-
ianism Scale also predicted real-life responses in both the 
real-life dilemma sample, standardized OR = 1.45, z = 2.35, 
p = .019, and the within-subject sample, standardized OR = 
1.60, z = 2.47, p = .014. The Impartial Beneficence subscale 
was not predictive in the real-life dilemma sample, stan-
dardized OR = 0.84, z = -1.15, p = .251, but it did predict 
decisions in the within-subject sample, standardized OR = 
1.54, z = 2.25, p = .024. 
Commentary Analyses   
Several commentaries suggested additional analyses to 
conduct. Evans and Brandt (2019) conducted an interaction 
analysis to investigate whether moral preferences (as mea-
sured through the hypothetical dilemma battery) more 
strongly predicted judgment on the hypothetical mouse 
dilemma than on the real-life mouse dilemma. Their analy-
sis was motivated by the finding that moral preferences 
predicted hypothetical but not real-life decisions. Our new 
data directly addresses this issue by demonstrating that 
moral preferences do modestly but significantly predict 
real-life decisions. While the interaction analysis remains 
informative, its importance is diminished as finding that 
moral preferences relate more strongly to hypothetical than 
to real-life decisions would be unsurprising in and of itself. 
We combined the data from all studies and conducted 
a linear mixed model analysis as the most powerful test. 
We included a random intercept for Study, and participants’ 
approval of utilitarian and deontological responses (as 
measured through the hypothetical moral dilemma bat-
tery), dilemma modality, and the interactions between ap-
proval and modality as predictors. Note that the within-
subject sample contains both responses to the hypothetical

Discussion  
Do people respond similarly to text-based, hypothetical 
dilemmas as they do to real-life dilemma situations? Bostyn 
et al. (2018) uncovered both similarities and differences. 
Our current findings extend this understanding in impor-
tant ways. Most notably, the newly gathered data reveals 
significant associations between responses to hypothetical 
dilemmas and actions taken in a real-life situation. While 
these associations were not strong (which likely explains 
the null result in the original work), their consistency 
across multiple measures alleviates potential worries about 
hypothetical dilemma research being disconnected from 
real-life moral decision-making. This result also aligns with 
other recent work showing a similar association between 
hypothetical moral preference and real-life decisions when 
confronted with another real-life sacrificial dilemma 
(Bostyn et al., in press). These findings indicate that hy-
pothetical and real-life moral judgments are meaningfully 
connected: Preferences measured through hypothetical 
dilemmas systematically relate to actual behavior. Hence, 
too strong a skepticism regarding the utility of hypothetical 
dilemma research is unwarranted, and hypothetical scenar-
ios are useful tools for studying moral psychology. 
A simple comparison of overall response rates between 
hypothetical and real-life conditions also showed no differ-
ences in the types of decisions people favored, with simi-
lar proportions choosing sacrificial harm in both contexts. 
However, these results need to be interpreted with caution. 
Some participants in the real-life condition intended to 
make sacrificial choices but acted too late due to the time 
pressure, while others made “deontological” choices specif-
ically because they considered the choices of other partic-
ipants, a consideration that likely did not impact choices 
on the hypothetical scenario. Depending on how one ac-
counts for these participants, the data could support either 
the presence or absence of meaningful differences between 
hypothetical and real-life decisions. Given this ambiguity, 
we cannot draw firm conclusions about whether people’s 
overall response tendencies truly differ across modalities. 
This points to a fundamental difficulty in comparing hy-
pothetical and real-life moral decisions: the real-life con-
text inevitably introduces factors that influence decision-
making in ways that are difficult to match in hypothetical 
scenarios. Even if researchers were to attempt to incor-
porate similar elements into hypothetical scenarios, ques-
tions would arise about how to do so appropriately. For in-
stance, consider our finding that participants factored in 
others’ likely choices when making real-life decisions. How 
should such a consideration be incorporated into hypothet-
ical scenarios? Making this information prominent in a hy-
pothetical vignette might artificially inflate its importance, 
while mentioning it only briefly risks participants overlook-
ing a factor that emerges more naturally in real situations. 
Similar challenges also arise with time pressure: while a 
20-second deadline can be implemented in both contexts, 
responding to an unexpected real-life situation requires 
more cognitive processing than reading a hypothetical sce-
nario, making nominally identical time constraints func-
tionally different. Another example concerns the temporal 
relationship between action and consequence. In our real-
life dilemma, participants understood that pressing the 
button would redirect the shock from five mice to one 
mouse, but that the actual shock would occur when the 
timer reached zero rather than immediately upon button 
press. This introduces a delay between moral choice and its 
execution that may influence participants’ sense of agency 
and personal responsibility. An alternative design in which 
button presses cause immediate harm to the single mouse 
might yield a different response pattern. Importantly, while 
such differences are salient in real-life dilemmas, these dis-
tinctions are much more abstract when written down in 
a hypothetical dilemma. Subtle variations in how partici-
pants understand the causal chain between their actions 
and outcomes represent another dimension along which 
real-life implementations can differ from both hypothetical 
scenarios and from each other. 
These methodological issues highlight the complexity of 
making direct between-subjects comparisons of hypothet-
ical and real-life moral judgments. However, our within-
subject design offers an advantage in this regard, precisely 
because of how transparent the comparison between the 
modalities is. While between-subjects comparisons strug-
gle with matching contextual factors across conditions, the 
within-subject approach embraces the connection between 
the two modalities. Participants likely recognized they 
would encounter the same dilemma in real life that they 
had previously responded to hypothetically, creating a di-
rect link between their hypothetical judgment and subse-
quent real action. This sets up a strong test of consistency: 
Participants were aware of the connection and had every 
reason to maintain coherent responses across contexts. In-
deed, the 81% consistency rate demonstrates that most 
participants are able to accurately predict their own moral 
behavior when confronted with the same scenario in both 
contexts. 
Nevertheless, it is also revealing that 19% of participants 
changed their decision when confronted with the actual sit-
uation. Moreover, the asymmetrical pattern of switching, 
where participants who initially rejected sacrificial harm 
were substantially more likely to switch to accepting it in 
the real situation than vice versa, suggests that these 
changes reflect genuine shifts in moral judgment rather 
than measurement error or random variation. The within-

Bostyn et al. (2018), most notably by Evans and Brandt

preferences relate more strongly to hypothetical than to 
real-life decisions. The current manuscript shows that even 
with an expanded sample, we did not uncover much evi-

Plunkett and Greene (2019) argued against measuring 
both utilitarian and deontological preferences, suggesting 
that the field’s traditional approach of measuring only util-

script, we find that both preferences independently predict 
real-life decisions. This provides clear empirical support for 
including both measures, as each captures unique variance 
in moral behavior. Plunkett and Greene (2019) also sug-
gested combining utilitarian and deontological ratings into 
a single relative difference score. We find no empirical ben-
efit to doing so over treating both moral preferences as sep-
arate predictors. 
Limitations  
Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, our 
within-subjects design used a fixed order rather than coun-
terbalancing conditions. This choice was motivated by our 
interest in how well people can anticipate their own moral 
behavior. While counterbalancing would have yielded a 
cleaner design, it would have halved our sample size for 
what we considered the most theoretically important com-
parison (hypothetical to real-life). Given our resource con-
straints, we prioritized adequate statistical power for our 
primary research question over a fully counterbalanced de-
sign. We suspect that if participants first encountered the 
real-life dilemma and then completed the hypothetical ver-
sion, they would simply remember and draw on their actual 
experience, making this test less informative. 
Second, like hypothetical approaches, our real-life ap-
proach has important external validity limitations. Our 
real-life dilemma paradigm still represents an artificial lab-
oratory situation. Shocking mice in a laboratory setting 
bears little resemblance to the moral dilemmas people typ-
ically encounter in their daily lives. Still, we argue that 
when participants believe their choices will have actual 
consequences, this engages psychological processes that 
may be absent in purely hypothetical scenarios. While im-
portant artificialities remain, our real-life paradigm can of-
fer valuable complementary insights to existing hypotheti-
cal methods. 
Finally, a temporal asymmetry existed across conditions 
that may have influenced apparent predictive validity in 
both Bostyn et al. (2018) and our replication. In the hy-
pothetical-only samples, participants completed the trolley 
battery and mouse dilemma in the same session, poten-
tially encouraging consistency through demand character-
istics. In contrast, both the real-life sample and the within-
subjects sample had 1-2 weeks between these measures. 
This asymmetry may have contributed to stronger apparent 
predictive validity in the hypothetical condition. 
Conclusion  
The ambition of real-life dilemma research is not to di-
minish the utility of hypothetical dilemma research. In-
deed, our finding of consistent associations between hy-
pothetical preferences and real-life choices actively affirms 
their value. However, combining both approaches may still 
provide the most complete picture of moral decision-mak-
ing . Hypothetical scenarios can help identify general pat-
terns and test theoretical predictions, while real-life stud-
ies are needed to reveal how these patterns manifest when 
people face actual consequences. This is especially impor-
tant because real-life idiosyncrasies can lead people to re-
vise their moral decisions even under conditions that en-
courage consistency. As a result, to fully understand moral 
cognition, hypothetical research will need to be supple-
mented with studies of actual moral behavior. Bostyn et al. 
(2018), along with the current manuscript demonstrate that 
real-life sacrificial decisions can be systematically studied 
in laboratory settings, opening new methodological possi-
bilities for the field. Such real-life studies can help inform 
the development of ecologically valid hypothetical scenar-
ios, while hypothetical research can help identify which 
real-life situations would be most informative to study. 
This complementary approach not only reveals important 
aspects of moral cognition that might be missed by either 
method alone but also helps identify methodological chal-
lenges that need to be addressed as the field moves forward.
