Affective norms for emotional ambiguity in valence, origin, 
and activation spaces
Adrianna Wielgopolan1  · Kamil K. Imbir1 
Accepted: 24 April 2022 
© The Psychonomic Society, Inc. 2022
Abstract
We introduce the first tool to measure the emotional ambiguity on three bivariate spaces: valence (dimensions of positivity 
and negativity); origin (automaticity and reflectiveness); and activation (subjective significance and arousal). Our database 
consists of 2650 word stimuli, assessed by 1380 participants in total with the usage of Self-Assessment Manikin scales for 
each dimension. We show that the ambiguity of valence, origin, and activation may be successfully perceived and reported 
in a behavioral procedure. The study has allowed us to compute characteristics of each word for every emotional dimension, 
thus providing the category of intensity of ambiguity (low, moderate, or high). We also studied the curvilinear relationships 
between the dimensions. Possible usage, specifics, and limitations of our database are discussed.
Keywords Emotional ambiguity · W ord stimuli · Valence · Origin · Activation
Introduction
A single word can change everything. Apart from the lin -
guistic properties of words, this kind of stimuli might be 
characterized by a distinguishable affective load, signifi -
cantly influencing the person reading it. The studies of 
the emotional consequences of the emotion-laden words 
(e.g., on decision-making or the effectiveness of cogni -
tive functioning in general) allow connecting psychology 
and linguistics, broadening the knowledge of these two 
fields. The attempts to map the relations between those 
two disciplines created a need for huge sets of validated 
and reliable stimuli that could be used in studies. How -
ever, there still were no sets that were validated by the 
ambiguity perceived in emotions, admitting that some 
stimuli might not be unequivocal, but rather ambiguous 
in their nature.
According to the dimensional approach to emotions, 
there are constructs underlying every human affective 
experience (Osgood et al., 1957; Russell, 2003); in other 
words, we might take each of our categorical states (e.g., 
fear, anger, happiness) and describe it by marking it 
somewhere on the spectrum of a particular dimension 
(e.g., valence or arousal; Russell, 1980). Because of that, 
it is possible to not only categorize emotions by their 
mutual characteristics (e.g., allowing for generalizing 
the study results not just to the discrete emotional cat -
egory like anger, but rather a group of emotions high on 
the dimension of arousal), but to also track the patterns 
between the dimensions and work on their intensity. The 
dimensions that we considered in the relevance to the 
structure of emotional experience were positivity and 
negativity (creating the space of valence), arousal and 
subjective significance (creating the space of activation), 
automaticity and reflectiveness (creating the space of 
origin).
The most intuitive and basic – thanks to its evolution -
ary meaning – is the dimension of valence (pleasant -
ness vs unpleasantness). It is very much connected to 
the reactions of approaching and withdrawal; the valence 
gives us information on whether something is positive 
and we should get closer to it, or whether it is nega -
tive and we should avoid it in order to not come to any 
harm (Clore, 1994; Frijda, 1994; Russell, 1980). There is 
an ongoing debate regarding whether the positivity and 
negativity are two rather orthogonal (though correlated 
with each other) dimensions, or whether they constitute a 
single spectrum (with negativity and positivity being two 
ends of it; Berrios et al., 2014; Russell & Barrett, 1999;

Vaccaro et al., 2020). The problem with the spectrum 
hypothesis is the fact that it only allows an emotional 
state to be marked on it in one place at a time: there is no 
possibility to be feeling good and bad at the same time. 
The middle of the spectrum means neutrality.
Finally, there are two different kinds of activation 
mechanisms (Imbir, 2016a, Imbir et al.,  2017), which we 
included in our study. First of them was described by Rus -
sell as the second most important and fundamental after 
valence, is arousal (Russell, 1980, 2003; Watson et al., 
1999), understood as the amount of an energy directed 
towards some object. Emotional arousal allows us to 
automatically process the situation and choose the most 
appropriate reaction to the stimuli (Imbir et al., 2017). 
The object described as being low on the arousal dimen-
sion would cause no or very little activation – the costs 
of ignoring it would probably also be low. However, the 
object high on the dimension of arousal would start an 
automatic, fast, and usually biological reaction to it.
The importance of objects (and also their relevance 
to the individual’s goals, values, principles and desires; 
a sort of regulative weight of a stimulus) is described 
by the dimension of subjective significance (Imbir, 
2015, 2016a, 2016c; van Hooff et al., 2008). The object 
assessed as being low on the subjective significance 
dimension would elicit no or very little reaction; it would 
be considered unimportant. The object high on the sub-
jective significance dimension would engage the indi-
vidual in some action, activating the cognitive, reflective 
processing of the object and the situation, and leading to 
the rational, thoughtful reaction.
The dual dimension of emotional origin was intro-
duced by Jarymowicz and Imbir (2015); it divided emo-
tional states into those coming from the heart and from 
the mind. The heart, being the metaphor for an automatic 
origin, meant an effortless, fast, and biological response 
(usually to rather simple, external stimuli; Imbir, 2016c ; 
Imbir & Jarymowicz, 2013; Jarymowicz & Imbir, 2015a). 
On the other hand, the mind, meaning the reflective ori-
gin, elicited the states which required cognitive, effortful 
processing, usually connected to comparing some val-
ues, knowing some rules or applying previous knowledge 
(Imbir, 2016d).
The ambiguity: Six dimensions, three spaces
The duality of presented dimensions (especially visible for 
the valence and origin) raises a question: is each of them in 
fact a univariate spectrum with two ends, or rather a bivari-
ate emotional space of relatively independent variables? Is 
there a possibility of co-occurrence of two opposite dimen-
sions (e.g., positivity and negativity) at the same time?
The Evaluative Space Model (ESM; Cacioppo & Bernt-
son, 1994, 1999; Norris et al., 2010) describes such a situa-
tion, postulating the relevant independence of positive and 
negative effects, which might be elicited both at the same 
time, creating an emotional state called mixed emotions. 
ESM conceptualizes the bivariate space of dimensions, built 
from the two spectra of positivity and negativity. Every emo-
tional state might be put somewhere in this space of valence, 
being described by two dimensions and possibly having dis-
tinctive characteristics on both of them (e.g., being high on 
the positivity and also high on the negativity). The ESM 
explains and describes the emotional ambivalence.
Furthermore, the recently introduced emotional-ambigu-
ity hypothesis describes not only the possibility of two dif-
ferent dimensions to be activated at the same time (Brainerd, 
2018; Mattek et al., 2017), but also its possible relationships 
with other variables (namely the arousal). It shows how the 
relation between valence and arousal might be influenced by 
the additional variable being the ambiguity (conceptualized 
as the uncertainty of the perceived valence; Brainerd, 2018); 
namely, the relation weakened within the higher states of 
uncertainty. This research is an example of how important 
it is to study ambivalence – as it is an unique emotional state 
with its own specificity (Berrios et al., 2015; Vaccaro et al., 
2020), and though it is more rare than feeling an equivo-
cal, unidimensional affect (Grossmann & Ellsworth, 2017), 
it still remains a fascinating affective phenomenon with its 
own consequences (Brainerd et al., 2021; Fong, 2006; Pacilli 
et al., 2012; Rees et al., 2013 ; Rothman et al., 2017). Nev -
ertheless, just as the emotional experience and its conse -
quences might be described in a more detailed way with the 
use of dimensions other than valence (as the characteristics 
on different dimensions – and correlations between them 
– bring distinctive consequences; e.g., van Hooff et al., 2008; 
Imbir, 2016b, Imbir et al.,  2017), differentiating the mixed 
emotions states by the kind of ambiguity might be similarly 
useful. The results obtained by Brainerd (2018) were the first 
showing how important it is to include variables other than 
just valence in studying ambivalence; they showed how the 
intensity of ambiguity might change the relations between 
valence and arousal.
The recently proposed theory discusses the occurrence of 
ambiguity on emotional spaces other than valence, namely 
the space of origin (built by the aforementioned dimensions 
of automaticity and reflectiveness) and, by the extension, 
also on the space of activation (dimensions of subjective 
significance and arousal; Wielgopolan & Imbir, in review). 
The assumption of existence of emotional ambiguity on 
the origin and activation spaces was based on the most 
fundamental properties of dual process theories, describ-
ing the two modes of processing (Kahneman, 2013; Strack 
et al., 2004). These two modes – the intuitive, automatic 
one, and the deliberative, reflective one – can exchange

tasks between themselves, cooperate and interchange when 
needed (Epstein, 2003). However, it is possible to imagine 
a state that could have both characteristics: a mixed feel -
ing of rationality (affective experience high on the reflective 
dimension) and emotionality (high on the automatic dimen-
sion) interlacing and co-occurring at the same time (Wiel -
gopolan & Imbir, in review). Following this thought, the 
possibility of activation ambiguity also arises – as subjective 
significance and arousal may be very much tied to, respec -
tively, the automatic and the reflective origin (Imbir et al., 
2017). An example of such an affective state might be the 
situation in which the activation is felt as both the automatic, 
instinctive, and general arousal (e.g., overwhelming anger) 
and reflective, cognitive subjective significance (e.g., deter-
mination to process situation and come up with a solution; 
Wielgopolan & Imbir, in review). For that reason, it seems to 
be especially interesting to not only add the space of origin 
(and the ambiguity on it being a result of two modes of pro-
cessing) to the already existing idea of ambivalence, but also 
to study and introduce the concept of the space of activation 
divided into two kinds, being a consequence of two different 
types of processing (automatic and reflective). This kind of 
ambiguity could serve as an explanation to the action that 
people take in reaction to the external stimuli; whether they 
decide to trust their guts and start the automatic response, 
whether they prefer reflective and effortful processing and 
thoughtful reaction, or whether they feel like taking up both 
of them at the same time. Our rationale for this space of 
activation is very similar to the one that we described for 
the space of origin; we would like to study the interlacing 
of the two opposite characteristics, two dimensions rooted 
in the dual process theories. Two kinds of activation might 
lead to very different reaction (fast and biological arousal 
vs. slower, cognitive subjective significance); the feeling of 
being torn between them might be a state of its own, interest-
ing characteristics.
We have already mentioned the fact that these two new 
spaces of emotional ambiguity – creating the model of ambi-
guity consisting of three bivariate spaces – might contribute 
to a more precise mapping (and, therefore, understanding) 
of an emotional experience. We expect those three spaces 
to be correlated with each other (as the bipolar dimensions 
were as well; Imbir, 2016a; Imbir et al., 2020) and create a 
model with the hierarchical organization (as the two kinds 
of activation come from two types of origin): with valence 
being the most fundamental one, through origin and until 
activation. It seems that this kind of model might be a better 
fit to the data (as it includes emotional variables having sig-
nificant consequences not only in emotional functioning, but 
also in cognitive processes; Hinojosa et al., 2010; van Hooff  
et al., 2008; Imbir et al., 2017, 2018, 2020, 2021). By study-
ing the valence, origin, and activation, we can measure all of 
the most fundamental properties of an object which elicited 
those assessments: we can see whether it is good or bad 
(valence), we can see whether we need to apply some action 
towards it (activation; furthermore, in our approach we also 
distinguish what kind of action would that be: generate by an 
automatic vs. reflective processing) and we can see whether 
we evaluate it with our heart or mind (origin). Potentially, 
the model built with three bivariate spaces might explain a 
bigger part of the variance in the experienced mixed emo -
tions than models using only one kind of ambiguity and 
separate variables (Wielgopolan & Imbir, in review).
Another argument for distinguishing and studying ambi-
guities of origin and activation may be their potential con -
sequences – possibly significantly different from those of 
ambivalence. This need for further research generates the 
demand for a database of validated stimuli sensitive enough 
to capture the subtle phenomenon of emotional ambiguity 
and allow it to be studied.
Word stimuli
There have been various affective stimuli used for research 
concerning the emotions, including sets of validated pictures 
(e.g., Carretié et al., 2019; Kurdi et al., 2017; Lang et al., 
1997; Marchewka et al., 2014), movies (e.g., Larsen et al., 
2001; Maffei & Angrilli, 2019), music (Fuentes-Sánchez 
et al., 2021; Imbir & Golab, 2017), short texts (e.g., Imbir, 
2016b), and words (Imbir, 2015, 2016a; Moors et al., 2013; 
Vo et al., 2009; Wierzba et al., 2015). The most popular 
method is still using the pictorial stimuli; however, they 
might be a bit too literal in a way, not allowing to induce 
more abstract constructs in participants (e.g., there seems 
to be no way to successfully present the pictures conveying 
philosophical values such as morality). Therefore, it seems 
that in contrast to the pictures, stimuli of greater abstrac -
tion (e.g., short texts, words) might be a better fit for acti -
vating affective states that are more complex and involving 
the cognitive processing. Creating this space for abstraction 
is especially important when measuring aforementioned 
dimensions of origin (and, in consequence, activation, which 
are tied to the two types of processing; Imbir, 2015; Imbir 
et al., 2017). Reflective processing is very much depend -
ent on verbalization and language (Gawronski & Creighton, 
2013; Kahneman, 2011; Strack & Deutsch, 2004), and by 
that it requires a specific type of stimuli to measure it.
There are some already validated databases of word stim-
uli in different languages (Imbir, 2016a; Monnier & Syssau, 
2014; Montefinese et al., 2014; Riegel et al., 2015; Soares 
et al., 2012), using the dimensional approach and studying 
particular characteristics underlying the emotions, or the 
categorical approach – studying the emotions as monolith 
constructs (e.g., Wierzba et al., 2015). Regardless of the 
type of approach taken by the researchers, the main aim of 
creating these databases was establishing affective norms

for broad sets of materials (the mean intensity of a particular 
dimension or category perceived in each stimulus). With this 
normalization process, it was later possible to not only use 
validated stimuli in future research, but to also compose sets 
of stimuli differing in some properties, but controlling for 
others (e.g., creating a list of words significantly differing in 
arousal, but not in valence; Imbir, 2017a).
These databases all have good reliability and were suc-
cessfully used in further studies (e.g., Imbir et al., 2020; 
Montefinese et al., 2021). However, many of them report 
some interesting facts concerning usually either the neutral 
groups of stimuli, or those of moderate intensity on a given 
dimension: in these groups, two types of stimuli might be 
found, namely the ones with low and the ones with high 
standard deviations (Bradley & Lang, 1999; Imbir, 2016a; 
Riegel et al., 2015). The words with the low standard devia-
tions seem to be perceived similarly by participants; they 
are congruent. Nevertheless, the ones with high standard 
deviations might only appear as neutral or moderate because 
of opposite judgements neutralizing each other; they are the 
victim of conceptualizing the dimensions as single spectra 
of two ends (Barrett & Russell, 1998; Russell, 1980). They 
give the idea that in these stimuli – mostly the neutral/mod-
erate ones, but potentially also on different levels – there is 
some subset of very specific words: the ambiguous ones. 
They would be explained by the bivariate ambivalence intro-
duced in the ESM model (Cacioppo et al., 2011) and by the 
recently proposed model of three bivariate ambiguous emo-
tional spaces (Wielgopolan & Imbir, in review). These stim-
uli might not be as frequent as the equivocal and unidimen-
sional ones – which is understandable as the mixed emotions 
states are rather rare (Grossmann & Ellsworth, 2017) and 
require some level of emotional awareness to notice them 
(Lane & Schwartz, 1987) – however, they remain intriguing 
and could possibly create an entire new method for mapping 
and measuring emotional ambiguity and its consequences.
Aims and hypotheses
Our main aim of the present study was to create the first 
database of word stimuli categorized specifically by their 
ambiguity on three emotional spaces (introducing the phe-
nomenon of ambiguity on the spaces of origin and activa-
tion for the first time in empirical research). We expected to 
obtain groups of word stimuli differing in the intensity of the 
perceived ambiguity in them.
We also wanted to explore the relationships between the 
studied dimensions and spaces. We expected to achieve 
similar results to previous studies showing the significant 
correlation patterns between emotional dimensions (e.g., 
Imbir, 2015, 1016); however, our approach included divid-
ing the dimensions (separating, for example, positivity and 
negativity), so it could give significantly more detailed infor-
mation about the structure of emotional experience.
Method
Participants
In total, 1405 volunteers took part in our study; however, 
data from 25 of them had to be excluded from our analy -
ses due to their not meeting the criteria of recruitment (the 
age range) or due to the care taken in ensuring the qual -
ity of the data (deleting the participants with monotonous 
answers for more than 15 words in a row, and those being 
outliers because of their overall response time). Therefore, 
we analyzed the data from 1380 individuals (701 women, 
679 men), all being Polish native speakers and students at 
various Polish universities and aged between 18 and 35 (M = 
21.84, SD = 2.89). They were also all users of the Facebook 
platform, where they received the invitation to our study, 
posted mostly on pages dedicated to student unions, inform-
ative groups of particular faculties and gathering students of 
different universities, colleges and academies.
Design
Our questionnaire was designed on the Qualtrics plat-
form with some programming modifications (JavaScript 
code snippets). The modifications were necessary in order 
to ensure that each of the 2650 words from our database 
(ANPW_R; Imbir, 2016) will be presented to the partici -
pants exactly the same number of times (26). Furthermore, 
we programmed the procedure so that it created a fully 
random list for each participant; every person got a list of 
exactly 50 words to assess six times (on six different scales: 
positivity; negativity; automaticity; reflectiveness; subjec-
tive significance; and arousal). This list was different for 
every person and each participant saw it six times (with 
randomized order of appearance for particular words) for 
six scales (corresponding to the aforementioned six dimen-
sions, and also appearing in randomized order). Showing 
all six dimensions to each person allowed us to create the 
individual ambiguity indicators, as we received the meas-
urements on opposite scales for one word from one person. 
Randomizing the order of showing the scales allowed us to 
minimize the anchor effect – participants were not biased by, 
for example, assessing the positivity of the words first and 
negativity second, or the other way around.

Materials
The words used in the study were all derived from the 
ANPW_R database (Imbir, 2016); for the purpose of our 
study, we have chosen only the nouns consisting of just 
one word. We have also excluded the names of the states or 
nations, resulting with the data set of 2650 words in total.
For assessing the word stimuli, we decided to employ 
both a written description of the scales and pictures to make 
sure that our participants understood the meaning of each 
emotional dimension and could conduct assessing at ease. 
We provided them with a detailed description of each scale 
(Imbir, 2014, 2016; Table  1) explaining the nature of each 
dimension (using synonyms, examples, and referring to the 
pictures) and its intensity by indicating the beginning and 
the end of the scale.
Furthermore, we put the pictures of simplified example 
emotional reactions (Self-Assessment Manikins, SAMs; 
Lang, 1980) right above the scale (being a slider, on which 
participants were supposed to indicate the emotionality of 
the particular word; sliders ranged from 0 to 100, and the 
intensity increased within the number). The pictures were 
derived from the original study (Lang, 1980) and further 
validation studies, including the measurements of origin, 
subjective significance and arousal (Imbir, 2015). For the 
purpose of the current study, we used scales containing 
three emotional pictures each (Fig.  1). In order to measure 
the ambiguity and convey the assumption of orthogonality 
of emotional dimensions, we have also divided the scales 
of valence and origin, creating four distinctive dimensions 
instead of two.
Procedure
Our participants were invited to complete the Qualtrics ques-
tionnaire via either public post or private message on Face-
book platform. At the beginning, they saw the information 
about the aim of the study, authors’ affiliation, and predicted 
duration of the study. They were also ensured that data gath-
ered during the study would be anonymous and analyzed in 
groups for scientific purposes only. The instructions speci -
fied that participants could withdraw from the study at any 
moment without giving any reason. Afterwards, they gave 
their consent for participation in the study (by checking one 
of the boxes on the screen, indicating that they understood 
the instructions and still wanted to take part in the study).
Each participant saw the list of 50 words on six scales. 
Each scale was presented on a separate page. At the top 
of the page, participants were provided with short instruc -
tions explaining that their task was to assess words on dif -
ferent emotional dimensions. The instructions also specified 
that there are no good and bad answers, and that we are

Results
Obtained data were aggregated and prepared for further 
analyses. Firstly, we computed mean assessments on six 
scales for each word and calculated the descriptive sta -
tistics (mean, standard deviation, median, minimum and 
maximum values) for each dimension. We computed the 
indicator of ambiguity for each of the three emotional 
spaces (by adding the ratings from each participant on 
two dimensions to each other – acquiring a measure of 
distance between the ratings – and then getting the mean 
value of it for every word, being the intensity of ambigu -
ity) and also calculated their descriptive statistics. All of 
the aforementioned values may be found in the Table  2.
We checked for the reliability of our participants’ 
judgements by employing a half-split method. We 
divided the whole data set into two subsets (using the 
numbering of participants in chronological order in 
which they completed the study; we put all of those 
with even numbering into the first group and all of those 
with uneven numbering into the second group; we also 
kept the 50:50 ratio of women and men in the two sam -
ples) and correlated them (Pearson’s r coefficient) using 
Spearman–Brown correction. We obtained significant 
and strong correlations for all of the dimensions, namely 
for: positivity ( r = .91, p < .001), negativity ( r = .96, p 
< .001), automaticity ( r = .87, p < .001), reflectiveness 
(r = .81, p < .001), subjective significance ( r = .92, p < 
.001), and arousal ( r = .92, p < .001) Fig.  2.
Calculating the descriptive statistics allowed us to search 
for general patterns between the dimensions. To check for 
the existing relationships, we correlated them with the usage 
of Spearman’s rho correlation (due to the lack of the nor -
mality of distribution for particular dimensions). All of the 
correlations are presented in the Table  3
Furthermore, based on the previous studies, we checked 
for the relationships between the particular dimensions. The 
estimation of the regression model (with valence dimensions 
being the independent variable and other dimensions always 
put as dependent variables) showed that for all of our studied 
variables, the cubic regression (polynomial model) was best 
fitted to the data, explaining the highest percentage of the 
variance (significantly higher than the linear and quadratic 
regressions1). We present all of the models’ coefficients in 
Appendix 2 (table: “Cubic regressions”), whilst the visuali-
zation of the relationships are in Fig.  3.
Using the medians of each dimension, we were also able 
to create the categories of ambiguity for each emotional 
space. All of the words with assessments on either of two 
opposite dimensions being lower than the median are in the 
0 category – meaning low ambiguity; among them we dis -
tinguish words of low assessments on two dimensions (“0”) 
and words being unidimensional with ratings above median 
on one out of two dimensions for affective space (marked 
as 0.1 and 0.2 for each of the corresponding dimensions). 
All of the words with two corresponding ratings (dimen -
sions of one space) above the median were further divided 
with the use of quartiles. Words with two assessments in 
the third quartile (higher than median) were categorized 
as those of moderate ambiguity (category 1 in the Appen -
dix 1), and those in the fourth quartile (highest 25% of the 
answers on both dimensions) were categorized as those of 
high ambiguity. With this procedure, we obtained subsets 
of words ambiguous (in different intensity) on all three of 
our proposed emotional spaces. By our criteria, there were 
exactly 321 words ambiguous on the space of valence (236 
of moderate ambiguity, 85 of high ambiguity), 468 on the 
space of origin (357 of moderate and 111 of high ambigu -
ity) and 950 on the space of activation (723 of moderate 
ambiguity and 227 of high). As presented on scatterplots in 
Fig.  4 (and as may be seen by the categories of ambiguity in 
Appendix 1), words ambiguous on one dimension can still 
be characterized by various properties on other dimensions: 
e.g., in the subset containing words of ambiguous origin, 
presented in panel B of Fig.  4, we can see the full spectrum 
of valence and arousal.

Discussion
We wanted to create a database as an extension of the 
ANPW_R (Imbir, 2016) with one general difference: our 
study aimed to shift the focus from the properties of the 
separate dimensions onto the intensity of an emotional ambi-
guity; we wanted to validate a data set enabling research into 
the very unique state of mixed emotions on different emo -
tional spaces (Cacioppo et al., 2011; Cacioppo & Berntson, 
1999; Wielgopolan & Imbir, in review). By showing the 
specificity of the words of ambiguous assessments on three 
spaces (valence, origin, and activation) we propose a new 
paradigm to measure the ambiguity perceived in language 
stimuli.
Our most important discovery is the very fact that our 
participants did see the different ambiguities in words. The 
minimal values on each of the dimensions were low, but they 
were not zero; similarly, the maximum assessments were 
high, but they did not reach the end of the scale, being 100 
(see Table  2). That means that people were able to perceive 
and assess the affective load of the words in an ambigu -
ous way. This phenomenon is obviously best visible in the 
group of our ambiguous stimuli (on different spaces), but 
it is worth mentioning that our method – showing partici -
pants the same words on different dimensions presented 
in random sequence – allowed us to obtain mixed results 
even for generally unidimensional and equivocal stimuli. 
This was in line with our predictions. Similarly, in some 
previous studies, people saw the ambivalence even in the 
most basic emotional categories, which until now were seen 
as utterly unidimensional (An et al., 2017). It seems that, 
however strong, confusing ambiguity might be a rather rare 
phenomenon, the subtle form of it may be found in most 
stimuli. This could also suggest that people might be signifi-
cantly better at noticing the mixed emotional states – in this 
case, in perceiving the different properties in stimuli – than 
we thought (Lane & Schwartz, 1987; Vaccaro et al., 2020). 
Perhaps the main reason for that is choosing the assessment 
method, which is intuitive enough (SAMs; Lang, 1980) and 
provides a wide spectrum of answers (a scale from 0 to 100), 
thus allowing for exact and sensitive measurement.
Furthermore, we were able to categorize our word stimuli 
into those of low, moderate, and high ambiguity on each of 
our three emotional spaces, therefore creating a database 
of stimuli for potential use in research concerning the psy -
chology of emotions and psycholinguistics. The fact that the 
stimuli ambiguous on one dimension were still varying on 
the others (see Fig.  4 and Appendix 1) ensures that creating 
a list with one variable differing in its level and controlling 
for other variables is possible. This allows the different con-
sequences for particular type and intensity of the ambiguity 
to be studied.
We also checked for correlations between our variables. 
They were similar to those obtained in the previous stud -
ies (Bradley & Lang, 1999; Imbir, 2015, 2016) even taking 
into consideration the fact that we did divide into two some 
of the dimensions usually studied as one (e.g., we divided 
the dimension of valence into dimensions of positivity and 
negativity). First of all, we showed the correlations between 
dimensions building our emotional spaces. Positivity and 
negativity were significantly and negatively correlated 
with each other; however, it is worth noticing that the cor -
relation was strong, but it was not perfect. Therefore, we 
might assume that positivity and negativity are each other’s 
opposites, but they do not completely exclude each other. 
Similarly, automaticity and reflectiveness were negatively 
correlated with each other; this time the correlation was 
moderately strong. On the other hand, the subjective sig -
nificance and arousal were positively correlated with each 
other; this relationship was also moderately strong. Perhaps 
that means the space of subjective significance and arousal 
might be more monolithic than the other two spaces; this 
property should definitely be taken into consideration while 
studying the consequences of different types of ambiguity. 
We expected the automaticity and reflectiveness to underlie 
the dimensions of (respectively) subjective significant and 
arousal; however, these pairs of dimensions were weakly 
correlated with each other. A possible explanation for this 
discrepancy might be the fact that – taking into considera -
tion that we measured six dimensions – there was some 
more dynamics in relations between variables than we pre -
dicted. Namely, the automaticity is correlated with valence 
dimensions, but reflectiveness has a very weak correlations 
with them; our participants clearly associated automatic -
ity with valenced affective states, whilst the reflectiveness 
was rather rated as “neutral”, independent from valence. As 
the assigned valence of an object is connected with arousal 
(Russell & Barrett, 1999), this specificity might influence 
the correlational relations between activation and origin. 
Possibly, these results were also caused by some cultural 
factors – the fact that our participants did not only see reflec-
tiveness as non-valenced, but also non-activating, independ-
ent from dimensions of activation.
We also fitted a regression line to the dimensions, trying 
to check for the relationships obtained in previous studies 
(Imbir, 2016a; Montefinese et al., 2014); we were especially 
interested in replicating the quadratic function of arousal 
and valence. However, although the models using the quad-
ratic functions were statistically significant (Appendix 2), in 
our study the best-fitted model (not only for the arousal and 
valence dimensions, but also for other relationships) was the 
cubic one. We considered this result as either an artefact (it

relationship from the cubic one. Nevertheless, the general 
relationships between variables seem the same as in previ -
ous studies (e.g., Imbir, 2016a; Montefinese et al., 2014), 
with the only differences coming from the specific method 
of measurement.
We showed that whilst our stimuli of different ambiguity 
levels still vary in their intensity of two other spaces (Fig. 4), 
there is some pattern for assessments of valence. Ratings of 
positivity were significantly higher than those of negativity 
for almost all categories of ambiguity; this might be a result 
of the positive-negative asymmetry in the positivity bias per-
spective (Frijda, 1988; Lewicka et al., 1992). The only group 
in which there was no difference was the one with stimuli 
of moderate ambiguity on activation space; additionally, we 
observed reversed effect for high ambiguity on activation 
space – in this category the negativity ratings were signifi -
cantly higher than positive ratings. This was probably caused 
by the relationship between valence and arousal, present in 
previous studies (Brainerd, 2018; Delaney-Busch et al., 
2016; Sass et al., 2010; Yao et al., 2016); highly negative 
words were highly arousing.
As for the limitations of the presented study, it is impor -
tant to remember that our participants were all young adults 
and students, thus composing a rather homogeneous group. 
Any future studies should make an attempt to compare 
whether there are any differences in the perception of ambi-
guity in words amongst young and older participants. This 
could be very possible when taking into account the fact that 
while aging we become more aware of our emotions (Lane & 
Schwartz, 1987); we also shift towards being more positive 
(suggesting the changes on the valence space), more reflec-
tive (potential differences on the space of origin), but also 
more passive (suggesting the differences in the perceived 
activation ambiguity; Ross & Mirowsky, 2008).
The homogeneity of our participants might also be seen 
in their ethnicity. It was inevitable for us to study only the 
Polish native speakers, as we had to make sure that they 
understood the stimuli without any difficulties. Nevertheless, 
any future studies should involve participants from other 
cultures, as there is some proof for cultural diversity in per-
ceiving and reporting ambivalence (An et al., 2017; Lomas, 
2017). We expect this effect to also be present for the spaces 
of origin and activation.
We have also proposed a method of categorization for the 
stimuli (assigning them scores 0–2 to describe the intensity 
of the ambiguity on particular dimensions). However, it is 
worth remembering that with that method the category of 
low ambiguity consists of both words of very low ambiguity 
(e.g., being a little bit positive and a little bit negative, cat -
egory 0) and unidimensional words (e.g., being highly posi-
tive and low negative; category 0.1). We distinguished those 
words by giving them different subcategories, however, this 
is a limitation of our categorization system. In further stud -
ies this issue should be clearly addressed and it should be 
marked whether the ambiguous words are compared with 
the unidimensional ones or the ones having low intensity 
of the ambiguity. It is important to notice that we provide 
a file containing all of the characteristics of words that we 
studied and therefore various sets of stimuli with particular 
properties might be easily composed. The ambiguity is a 
rather rare and subtle phenomena; it is difficult for people 
to actually experience mixed emotional states or perceive 
qualitatively opposite characteristics in one object, let alone 
to notice it and report (Barrett et al., 2000; Feldman Barrett, 
2017; Lane et al., 1990), so any groups of stimuli meant to 
be compared should be meticulously composed basing on 
their characteristics.
Finally, an important limitation of our study was the fact 
that it was only behavioral; we collected the assessments 
from participants via the Internet. Even from this validation 
study, we see that emotional ambiguity perceived in words 
is much less frequent than unambiguity. Therefore, it seems 
that for such a unique and subtle phenomenon other research 
methods should be employed; perhaps those methods that 
are more sensitive, prone to detect small changes not only 
in human behavior, but also in human biology. We believe 
that any future studies should involve mapping the electro -
physiological (with an EEG measurement) correlations of 
perceived ambiguity, as well as those of visual attention 
(eye-tracking measurement). That way we could not only 
study the consequences of different types of ambiguity, but 
potentially also the mechanisms underlying it.
Future studies should also consist of broadening the data-
base that we prepared so far. In the present research, we 
examined the three types of ambiguity perceived in Polish 
nouns; it was in line with previous studies (using usually 
nouns as stimuli; (e.g., Imbir et al., 2021; Montefinese et al., 
2021); it was also very much connected with theories con -
cerning the mixed emotional states felt towards an object 
(Cacioppo & Berntson, 1994; Larsen et al., 2001). However,

The data set description
All of our word stimuli are presented in Appendix 1 in Eng-
lish (column A) and Polish (column B). We also provide 
number of letters for Polish words (column C), mean rat-
ings for every word on each dimension (columns D, E, F, 
G, H and I respectively for dimensions of positivity, nega-
tivity, automaticity, reflectiveness, subjective significance, 
and arousal). We computed the mean intensity of ambigu-
ity for each word on three emotional spaces (columns J, K, 
and L, respectively, for the space of valence, consisting of 
positivity and negativity; space of origin, consisting of auto-
maticity and reflectiveness; and space of activation, con-
sisting of subjective significance and arousal) and assigned 
them a category that is the measure of ambiguity (0 – low, 
1 – moderate, 2 – high; columns M, N, and O, respectively, 
for valence, origin, and activation). Additionally, we distin-
guish words of low ambiguity into ones being ambiguous 
and unidimensional; the ambiguous ones are simply marked 
as “0”, whilst the unidimensional ones (having rating above 
the median for one dimension at given space) are marked as 
0.1 (for positivity, automaticity and subjective significance 
in corresponding columns: M, N, and O) and 0.2 (for nega-
tivity, reflectiveness and arousal in corresponding columns: 
M, N, and O).
