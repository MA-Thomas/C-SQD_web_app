The differential subcellular localization 
of soybean transcripts, an additional regulatory 
mechanism of gene activity
Sutton Tennant1,2†, Erik J. Amezquita1,2,3†, Yaohua Li1,2, Benjamin Smith4, 
Sai Subhash Mahamkali Venkata Subramanyam5, Sergio Alan Cervantes-Pérez5,7, Sandra Thibivilliers1,2,

Abstract 
Background: Our understanding of gene function is often driven by its expression 
and, a fortiori, based on its RNA abundance in a cell, a tissue, or an organ. This assump-
tion often neglects the limited correlation between RNA and protein abundance, 
largely due to post-transcriptional and pre-translational regulation. Among these 
regulatory processes, the spatial distribution of RNA molecules within cells has been 
reported as a major contributor of cellular function in microbial and animal systems. 
However, our understanding of the differential cellular distribution of transcripts 
in plants is very limited.
Results: In this manuscript, using Molecular Cartography™ and Xenium, two high-
resolution and sensitive spatial transcriptomic technologies, we comprehensively 
analyze the differential mapping of millions of plant transcripts in the nuclear and cyto-
plasmic compartments of various soybean nodule cell types. Our analysis reveals 
distinct distributions of transcripts between the nuclear and the cytoplasmic compart-
ments of the soybean nodule cell. We also detect variability in cytoplasmic distribution 
among transcripts encoded by different genes and across cell types.
Conclusions: Our findings reveal the strong diversity in the spatial distribution of tran-
scripts in and between differentiated plant cells. It suggests that transcript localization 
serves as an additional regulatory layer beyond transcriptional control. By modulating 
nuclear export and cytoplasmic positioning, plant cells may fine-tune translational 
efficiency and gene function. This study underscores the importance of incorporating 
spatial information into transcriptomic analyses and provides new insights into the reg-
ulatory architecture of plant RNA biology.

Background
Multicellular eukaryotic organisms exhibit a remarkable level of cellular heterogeneity 
crucial for their development and growth [1, 2]. This cellular heterogeneity is, in part, 
the result of the differential activity of the genes under the control of genetic and epi -
genetic regulatory mechanisms and, in part, the product of various post-transcriptional 
and pre-translational mechanisms. For instance, alternative splicing triggers the differ -
ential interaction of messenger RNAs (mRNAs) with proteins such as mRNA binding 
proteins (RBPs) [3], ribosomal subunits [4], and mRNA degradation proteins [5]. These 
interactions affect mRNA stability, storage, decay, translation, and localization [6]. RNA 
folding also impacts the recruitment of ribosomal complexes, affecting translational ini -
tiation, elongation, and efficiency [7, 8]. RNA methylation further contributes to mRNA 
stability and translation [9]. These regulatory processes work in tandem to control pro -
tein translation and cell function. While these processes have been reported in plants 
[10–12], the distribution of mRNAs in the cell as an additional mechanism to control 
gene and protein function has been less explored.
Our understanding of differential mRNA localization, its impact on the spatial and 
temporal regulation of protein production, and its implication in cell biology mainly 
stems from research in yeast, bacterial, and animal species [13, 14]. For example, over 
30 mRNAs are known to be localized to specific subcellular regions of Saccharomyces 
cerevisiae cells. Among them, the ASH1P mRNA controls mating-type switching notably 
through its localization to the bud tip of dividing mother cells, a sub-cellular compart -
ment where the ASH1P transcripts are localized and retained in the daughter cell after 
cell division [15, 16]. In bacteria, mRNA translation was long thought to occur co-tran -
scriptionally, and mRNA localization was not considered to be a feature of bacterial gene 
expression [14]. However, with the technological advancement of mRNA visualization 
assays, bacterial transcriptomes are now known to be heavily spatiotemporally regu -
lated. For instance, substantial fractions of the Escherichia coli transcriptome, including 
membrane mRNAs, polar mRNAs, and regulatory sRNAs, are not randomly localized in 
the bacterium. Notably, a strong correlation between mRNA protein localizations and 
functions has been noticed [17–19].
Messenger RNA subcellular localization has also been studied in several animal spe -
cies [14, 20, 21] such as Xenopus laevis [22–25] and Drosophila melanogaster, where 
mRNA localization is critical for cell fate determination and the creation of cell line -
ages during embryogenesis [6, 13, 26, 27]. In Gallus domesticus, β-actin mRNA is local -
ized near the leading edge of fibroblast cells, causing efficient actin polymerization and 
forward protrusion [28]. In Mus musculus and Rattus norvegicus, mitochondrial protein 
mRNAs are actively transported and localized near the mitochondria of the cell [29–35].
Comparatively, mRNA localization is less studied in plants, with research mainly 
focusing on three species: rice, Arabidopsis, and tobacco [36, 37]. In rice, studies focus 
mainly on the spatial association of glutenin and prolamin mRNAs to protein bodies 
and the endoplasmic reticulum in endosperm cells, contributing to efficient protein 
synthesis and protein body formation and storage [38–45]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, the 
relationship between nuclear vs. cytoplasmic localization of mRNA populations in the 
presence of stress was reported as a mechanism to accurately control protein translation

[46–49]. Tobacco plants have been mostly used to test and develop novel methods to 
study mRNA localization in plant cells [50–53].
Recent developments in spatial transcriptomic technologies and their application to 
various plant organs allow the concomitant visualization of multiple transcript popula -
tions at the cellular and subcellular levels. Here, using high-resolution and high-sensitiv-
ity Resolve Biosciences Molecular Cartography ™ imaging-based spatial transcriptomic 
technology, we captured the subcellular spatial distribution of large populations of 
soybean and bacterial transcripts at a subcellular resolution in the soybean nodule, a 
root organ characterized by the symbiotic relationship existing between the plant cells 
Bradyrhizobium diazoefficiens, nitrogen-fixing soil bacteria. This study represents a 
significant increase in the size of the transcript populations analyzed compared to pre -
viously used in situ hybridization studies in plants [54, 55]. Our computational and top-
ological analysis of the distribution of transcripts in the nodule cells revealed differential 
subcellular patterns across genes in the same cell and across cell types for the same tran-
script population. In addition to revealing the heterogeneity existing in the subcellular 
distributions of plant transcripts, our work reveals potential novel connections between 
gene function, cell state, and mRNA subcellular localization.

Evaluation of the transcriptomic depth of Molecular Cartography™ technology
Our previous work using Molecular Cartography ™ technology led to the annotation of 
the different clusters of the soybean root and nodule [56] and confirmed the remark -
able sensitivity, specificity, and resolution of this technology in detecting individual tran-
scripts in an organ’s cross-section. Hence, in addition to nicely supporting this functional 
annotation, we noticed that Molecular Cartography ™ revealed differential patterns of 
subcellular localizations of soybean transcripts between populations of transcripts and 
between cell types.
Prior to further exploring this differential distribution of plant transcripts between cell 
compartments, we first estimated the depth of the transcriptomic coverage of Molecular 
Cartography™. To do so, we compared the number of nuclear transcripts encoded by 76 
soybean genes using Molecular Cartography ™ technology with our previously published 
single-nuclei RNA-seq (sNucRNA-seq) dataset (Additional file 1: Table S1) [56]. These 
genes were selected based on the detection of their transcripts using both Molecular 
Cartography™ and sNucRNA-seq technologies in 1,162 and 7,830 nodule nuclei, respec -
tively. Our analysis revealed that Molecular Cartography ™ detected almost eight times 
more transcripts compared to sNucRNA-seq technology (Fig.  1A, Additional file  1: 
Table  S1). This difference results, at least for a part, from the limited capture of tran -

Differential compartmentation of soybean transcripts between the nuclear 
and the cytoplasm of different nodule cell types

tures. Among them, those infected by rhizobia are endoreduplicated to support the

variations existing between nodule cell types, we calculated the nuclear and cytoplasmic 
densities of plant transcripts by first implementing a Euclidean distance transformation

the transcript coordinate information captured by Molecular Cartography™ (Fig. 1C and 
D). To gain more meaningful results, we restricted our analysis to the genes expressed in

Our analysis revealed multiple statistically significant differences in transcript locali -
zation between the nucleus and the cytoplasm and between different populations of 
transcripts in the same cell type (Fig.  3). While most transcripts were identified in both 
nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments [e.g., Glyma.17G195900 and Glyma.15G210100 
in the rhizobia-infected cells of the nodule (red and yellow, respectively); Fig.  3B], 
a few populations of transcripts were exclusively localized in the cytoplasm [e.g., 
Glyma.05G092200 in the infected cells of the nodule (green); Fig.  3B]. This result sug -
gests that plant cells are capable of differentially regulating the allocation of transcripts 
between the nucleus and the cytoplasm. For instance, the rhizobia-infected cell of the 
soybean nodule differentially regulates the allocation of their transcripts between the 
nucleus and the cytoplasm (nuclear vs. cytoplasmic distribution of Glyma.17G195900 
vs. Glyma.15G210100 transcripts, p-value = 1.31e−21; nuclear vs. cytoplasmic distribu -
tion of Glyma.17G195900 vs. Glyma.05G092200 transcripts, p-value = 3.51e−51; nuclear 
vs. cytoplasmic distribution of Glyma.15G210100 vs. Glyma.05G092200 transcripts, 
p-value = 1.38e−82) (Fig.  3A). Similarly, differential nuclear vs. cytoplasmic transcript 
localization was also noticeable in the vascular bundle cells of the nodule (Fig.  3A). For 
instance, the nuclear vs. cytoplasmic distributions differ between the Glyma.11G078300 
(Fig.  3C, red dots) and the Glyma.06G256000 transcripts (Fig.  3C, yellow dots) 
(p-value = 7.42e−4, Fig. 3A). However, the distribution in these two compartments of the 
transcripts encoded by Glyma.11G078300 (Fig.  3C, red dots) and Glyma.15G245800 
(Fig. 3C, green dots) genes are not significantly different (p-value = 1.0; Fig. 3A). A sim-
ilar conclusion also applies to the transcripts of genes expressed in the soybean nod -
ule’s inner/outer cortex cells (Fig.  3D) and uninfected cells in the infection zone of the 
soybean nodule (Fig.  3E). For example, in the inner/outer cortical cells, transcripts 
encoded by the Glyma.20G061300 (Fig.  3D, yellow dots) and Glyma.06G259400 genes 
(Fig. 3D, red dots) are not differentially distributed between the nucleus and the cyto -
plasm (p-value = 0.7; Fig. 3A) but those encoded by the Glyma.20G061300 (Fig.  3D, yel-
low dots) and Glyma.12G027400 (Fig.  3D, green dots) are (p-value = 4.99e −13; Fig.  3A). 
In the uninfected cells in the infection zone of the soybean nodule (Fig.  3E), statistically 
significant differences exist between the distributions of the transcripts encoded by the 
Glyma.06G00200 (Fig.  3E, yellow dots) and Glyma.05G092200 genes (Fig.  3E, green 
dots) (p-value = 1.45e−2, Fig. 3A). Our results support the differential allocation of vari -
ous populations of transcripts between the nucleus and the cytoplasm. We hypothesize 
that these differential distributions might act as a post-transcriptional and pre-transla -
tional regulatory mechanism of protein biosynthesis to control plant cell biology.
Considering the above hypothesis, we comprehensively examined the functions of 
proteins and their association with nuclear vs. cytoplasmic distribution of their tran -
scripts. For instance, Glyma.15G210100 is highly expressed in the rhizobia-infected 
cells of the nodule. However, our analysis revealed that its transcripts are predominantly 
localized to the nucleus (Fig.  3A and B, yellow dots), suggesting their limited translation 
into proteins. This gene encodes a trehalose-6-phosphate synthase (TPS), an enzyme 
that produces trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P), a key intermediate in trehalose biosyn -
thesis (Barraza et al., 2016). This disaccharide plays a critical role during the symbiosis

between rhizobia and plant nodules because its dephosphorylated version is used by the 
bacterial symbiont as a reserve of carbon and energy [58, 59]. It also contributes to mit-
igating the impact of reactive oxygen species, biochemical compounds produced dur -
ing nitrogen fixation and assimilation, on biomolecules. Considering the central role 
of trehalose in the nodulation process, we hypothesize that the nuclear localization of 
Glyma.15G210100 transcripts may serve as a fast and efficient method of controlling 
and responding to the needs for trehalose biosynthesis by translocating these transcripts 
to the cytoplasm. Similarly, the vascular bundle-specific Glyma.06G256000 and inner/
outer cortex Glyma.20G061300 genes encode proteins carrying the NB-ARC, LRR, 
and TIR domains. These domains are critical components of plant immune receptors 
[60]. While expressed, our analysis revealed the preferential retention of the transcripts 
encoded by these two genes in the nucleus of the vascular bundle and cortical cells. We 
hypothesize that the nuclear localization of both these gene transcripts may trigger a fast 
and efficient response to pathogens in these cell types (Fig.  3C and D) [61]. The valida -
tion of these hypotheses would require an understanding of the mechanisms controlling 
transcript nuclear export prior to modifying the RNAs of targeted genes to modify their 
export.
To a lesser extent, we also noticed differential nuclear vs. cytoplasmic distribution

rhizobia-infected vs. -uninfected cells, p-value = 7.42e−4; Additional file  3: Fig. S1). 
Glyma.12G027400, a gene encoding a subunit of nucleolin, also shows differential 
nuclear vs. cytoplasmic localization patterns in the four nodule cell types [Additional 
file  3: Fig. S1; i.e., vascular vs. inner/outer cortical cells, p-value = 0.01; vascular vs.

Independent validation of the cellular and subcellular localization of soybean transcripts 
in nodule cells

ous nodule cell types as revealed using Molecular Cartography technology, we applied 
another spatial transcriptomic approach, 10 × Genomics Xenium technology, on 11 
soybean genes. Overall, we observed a strong concordance in the cellular and sub -
cellular distribution of transcripts between the two platforms. At the cellular lev -
els, we were able to confirm the preferential expression of Glyma.17G195900 and 
Glyma.05G216000 in the rhizobia-infected cells of the nodule, the activity of 
Glyma.18G062100, Glyma.08G125800, Glyma.11G078300, and Glyma.15G245800 in

Glyma.06G002000, Glyma.03G143700 (CYP93A1), and Glyma.16G039800 in the nodule 
cortical cells (Additional file 4: Fig. S2). At the subcellular level, we also confirmed the 
characteristic preferential localization of transcripts encoded by the Glyma19G255500 
and Glyma06G259400 genes in the nucleus of the cortical cells of the nodule (Fig.  3A,

localization of the Glyma.17G195900 transcripts in the rhizobia-infected cells of the

Comparing the outcome of both technologies, we also noticed that the level of expres -
sion of genes (i.e., intensity of the labelling of the transcripts) was similar between the 
two platforms, suggesting both qualitative and quantitative validation of gene expression 
patterns.
We took advantage of the embedding of 14, 28, and 42 DAI nodules for the Xenium 
experiment to observe potential changes in the expression pattern of the 11 selected 
genes at the cellular and sub-cellular levels. Several genes show a consistent expression 
pattern across the three developmental stages of soybean nodules. At the cellular lev -
els, Glyma.17G195900 and Glyma.05G216000 are consistently expressed in the rhizo -
bia-infected cells, although the expression of Glyma.05G216000 in the 14 DAI nodule 
is lower (Additional file 5: Fig. S3). A similar trend was observed in the vascular bundles 
for the Glyma.18G062100, Glyma.08G125800, Glyma.11G078300 genes, but not for the 
Glyma.15G245800 gene,  which shows a consistent expression level between the three 
developmental stages of the nodule (Additional file 5: Fig. S3). In the cortical cells of the 
nodule, we also observed similar expression levels between 14, 28, and 42 DAI nodules 
for the Glyma.19G255500, Glyma.06G259400, Glyma.06G002000, Glyma.03G143700 
(CYP93A1), and Glyma.16G039800 genes. At the subcellular level, we noticed that 
casein kinase transcripts encoded by the Glyma.17G195900 gene shifted from predomi -
nantly non-nuclear localization at 14 DAI to nuclear localization at 28 DAI (confirming 
our Molecular Cartography datasets) and 42 DAI nodules (Additional file 4: Fig S2), first 
panel, see the arrows associated with the infected cells). Such a pattern, without visibly 
affecting the population and distribution of the cytoplasmic transcripts, suggests a fine-
tuning of the post-transcriptional regulation of these transcripts during nodule develop -
ment, potentially highlighting its role in controlling protein phosphorylation.

Discussion
In this study, we leveraged high-resolution spatial transcriptomics technology, Molecu -
lar Cartography™, to further highlight the impact of the subcellular localization of tran -
scripts on plant cell biology. The use of 10 × Genomics Xenium technology on 28-days 
old soybean nodule cross sections further support our observations and gave us an 
opportunity to compare the advantages from two technologies. We found that these 
two technologies are capable to capture with limited noise the transcriptional pattern 
of genes at the tissue and cellular level. One advantage of 10 × Genomics Xenium tech -
nology is the concurrent analysis of the expression of up to 480 genes and for a lower 
cost per gene compared to Molecular Cartography ™. However, we found the Molecular 
Cartography™ precisely reveals the subcellular pattern of localization of the transcripts 
at a higher/subcellular resolution. We recommend that the selection of the use of one 
technology versus another should be driven by the scientific question to answer and/or 
the research hypothesis to verify.
Our Molecular Cartography ™ analysis revealed that the distribution of mRNAs 
between the nucleus and cytoplasm of the cell varies significantly across genes and 
cell types, suggesting that spatial transcript allocation is a critical post-transcriptional/
pre-translational regulatory mechanism. Additionally, upon being translocated to the 
cytoplasm, plant transcripts are differentially localized in the cytoplasm of the cell. 
This subcellular localization varies between populations of transcripts encoded by dif -
ferent genes. While the interpretation of these differential patterns and their impact 
on plant cell biology would require further analysis, including single-cell and subcel -
lular resolution proteomic approaches, our analysis reveals correlations between the 
patterns of localization of the transcripts encodes by a couple of soybean gene (i.e., 
Glyma.05G092200 and Glyma.17G195900 based on the intensity of hotspot of accu -
mulation of transcripts and number of transcripts) and the eccentric localization of the 
cell in the nodule. This eccentric configuration is like the pattern of senescence of rhizo -
bia-infected cells that begins at the center of the infection zone and gradually spreads 
outward toward the periphery [62, 63]. Considering the age of the nodule used to con -
duct our spatial transcriptomic analysis (28 days post-inoculation), we propose that the 
eccentric pattern of plant cells based on the number of transcripts and their density in 
hotspots is a molecular marker of the senescing state of the rhizobia-infected cells.
Additionally, our findings expand the implications of mRNA nuclear retention on 
animal and plant cell biology. Current literature focuses on the nuclear retention of 
transcripts as a mechanism to control translation during a period of stress or nitrate 
response [30, 49, 61]. Here, using a technology that offers a nanometer-level resolution, 
we provide evidence that events of nuclear retention are not restricted to responses to 
abiotic stress but would also occur in response to biotic stress. When considering the 
study of the differential mRNA subcellular localization in plants, previous studies using 
smFISH or in-situ hybridization reported valuable information but only for a handful of 
genes [37, 38, 40, 46, 49]. Here, we use a method enabling the characterization of the dis-
tribution of millions of plant transcripts generated from up to one hundred genes with 
an outstanding resolution.
Our analysis of hundreds of cells, especially the rhizobia-infected cells, and many dif -
ferent plant genes reveals the differential patterns of RNA sub-cellular localization. To 
Page 15 of 21
Tennant et al. Genome Biology            (2026) 27:9 
 
identify the regulatory mechanisms governing their differential, we propose to identify 
commonly shared mRNA sequence motifs between differentially localized transcripts 
in the same cell. We assume that such an analysis would require the co-assessment of 
transcript sub-cellular localization for hundreds or thousands of genes. Based on the 
hypothesis, identifying such regulatory RNA sequences could offer an opportunity to 
control transcript allocation in a cell [6]. Besides, to further demonstrate the biological 
impact of the differential sub-cellular localization of plant transcripts, the simultaneous 
capture of the localization of transcripts and the encoded protein will help reveal the 
role of RNA allocation in the cell.
Overall, our work shows clear differences in the sub-cellular localization patterns of 
soybean plants transcripts in both the nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments of the cell. 
We raise the limitation that as we increase our understanding of the plant transcriptome 
at an ever-increasing resolution, without a deeper understanding of mRNA sub-cellular 
localization and how it contributes to protein production, we cannot precisely predict 
protein status from transcriptomic analysis. A deeper understanding of mRNA locali -
zation in various plant species would allow us to make stronger connections between 
transcriptomic analysis predictions and actual cell protein production and potentially 
develop targeted interventions to impact mRNA localization and protein production at 
a single-cell level. To pursue this goal, we present a novel, high-throughput, and high-
resolution method of studying mRNA localization in both animal and plant tissues, pro -
viding a valuable means to fill our scientific gap in mRNA localization studies in plants 
and further increase our understanding of the central dogma of molecular biology.
Conclusions
Our application of Molecular Cartography ™ on soybean nodule tissue and our Image J 
pipeline analysis methods provide a high-throughput, unbiased, and novel approach to 
study transcript localization patterns in plant cells. Our results highlight an additional 
level of regulation of gene activity and protein biosynthesis at the post-transcriptional/
pre-translational level. Our work also highlights the importance of considering the spa -
tial distribution of transcripts when interpreting gene expression, its impact on pro -
tein production and cell function, and its potential role in better controlling unique cell 
states. For instance, it contributes to explaining the differences existing between a cel -
lular and a nuclear transcriptome when conducting single-cell and single-nucleus RNA-
seq experiments. Additionally, we showcase the untapped potential of high-resolution 
spatial transcriptomics for studying the spatial distribution of transcripts. We assume 
that such technology could also be used to explore RNA trafficking between plant cells.
Methods
Plant material
Soybean (Glycine max Williams 82) seeds were sterilized as described previously [64]. 
To generate and isolate mature nodules, 3-day-old seedlings were germinated on B&D 
agar medium without nitrogen and inoculated with a suspension of B. diazoefficiens 
USDA 110 (OD600nm = 0.1). After 72 h of incubation in the dark, the seedlings were 
transferred to vermiculite: perlite mix (3:1) and grown in the growth chamber (16  h 
Page 16 of 21Tennant et al. Genome Biology            (2026) 27:9 
light/8  h dark) at 20  °C–26  °C. Mature nodules were collected 28  days after bacterial 
inoculation (DAI) to perform Molecular Cartography™ Spatial Transcriptomics.
Tissue preparation/sectioning
Molecular Cartography experiments conducted as previously described [56] were used 
in this study. Shortly, 28 DAI nodules are fixed in 4% FPA-Triton X-100 (paraformal -
dehyde: ThermoFisher Cat# 043368.9L; Triton, Sigma Aldrich Cat# X-100) in PBS pH 
7.0 for 40 min at room temperature. Nodules are then dehydrated in ethanol gradients 
in 1-h increments on ice (15%EtOH > 30 > 50 > 70 > 80 > 90 > absEtOH). Nodules are 
then cleared in a series of Histo-Clear/EtOH gradients at 22ºC (100%EtOH, 25% Histo-
Clear/75% EtOH, 50% Histo-Clear/50% EtOH, 75% Histo-Clear/25% EtOH, 100% Histo-
Clear). Nodules are embedded in paraplast at 60ºC, and paraplast blocks are dried at 
37–42ºC overnight.
To conduct 10 × Genomics Xenium experiments, the largest  nodules were collected 
after 14, 28, and 42 DAI. Fresh nodules were fixed in 4% FPA-Triton X-100 (paraform -
aldehyde: ThermoFisher Cat# 043368.9L; Triton, Sigma Aldrich Cat# X-100) in PBS pH 
7.0 for 40 min at RT. Tissue is then incubated in 10% and then 20% sucrose solutions for 
2 h each (or until tissue sinks). Tissue is then embedded in OCT blocks. Nodule tissue 
sections were cut at a thickness of 10 µm using fresh microtome blades (crytostat, Leica 
CM3050 S; blades, Leica Biosystems Cat# DB80LX).
Generating transcript localization data from Molecular Cartography™
When analyzing the sub-cellular localization of transcripts using Molecular Cartog -
raphy technology, one 10  µm-thick cross-section of a soybean nodule embedded in 
paraffin was stained with DAPI and Calcofluor White to stain nuclei and cell walls, 
respectively. High-resolution Z-stack images of stained soybean nodule cross-sections 
were generated through confocal microscopy from Resolve Biosciences. The analysis of 
the microscopic images was conducted using custom-made macros (https:// github. com/ 
Llame ro/ Libau lt_ Trans cript_ Local izati on) [65, 66]. First, gridlines left over from the tile 
scans were removed by linear interpolation across the gap between tiles using the ImageJ 
macro "Fill grid lines." Parameters to define cell wall boundaries were identified by water 
shedding Gaussian blurred 16-bit Z-stack Calcofluor White images using the ImageJ 
macro "Segment Cells". Default values were used for the "Segment Cells" macro, includ -
ing Gaussian blur value = 20, Minimum/maximum watershed tolerance = 100/1000. 
Watershed tolerance values were set to minimize excessive segmentation of cells, and 
ideal parameters were saved for the final analysis macro. Count masks of the nuclei were 
generated from 16-bit z-stack DAPI-stained images using the ImageJ macro "Threshold 
Stack" using default parameters and manually setting thresholds for particle analysis fil -
ters for mean fluorescence, area of fluorescence, standard deviation of fluorescence, and 
circularity of fluorescent bodies. Thresholds were set for each filter to optimally define 
nuclei from all cell types, varying in shapes and sizes. Transcript coordinate data for 
all genes analyzed was provided by Resolve Biosciences. These genes were previously 
identified as markers of the nodule cell types [56]. Using the nuclear mask, defined cell 
segmentation parameters, and transcript coordinate data, the ImageJ macro "Distance 
Histogram By Cell" performed a Euclidean distance transform to distinguish nuclear and 
Page 17 of 21
Tennant et al. Genome Biology            (2026) 27:9 
 
cytoplasmic localized transcripts for all defined cells of the soybean nodule organ. Raw 
data generated from Image J analysis can be found on Zenodo [67].
When analyzing the sub-cellular localization of transcripts using Xenium technology, 
spatial imaging and transcript detection were performed using the Xenium Analyzer 
(10 × Genomics) on 3 different developmental stages of the nodule (i.e., 14, 28, and 42 
DAI), and, for each developmental stage, on 3 independent biological replicates with a 
custom gene expression panel. Xenium probing and reading generated high-resolution 
morphology images (DAPI-stained nuclei, OME-TIFF format) and spatially resolved 
transcript coordinate files (CSV/Parquet). Regions of interest (ROIs) were subsequently 
defined within Xenium Explorer (10 × Genomics), and the corresponding morphology 
images and transcript datasets were exported for downstream analysis.
Nuclear vs. cytoplasmic localization of transcripts cross nodule cell types and genes
Cells identified to have a nucleus and assigned a unique cell type were used to conduct 
a comparative analysis of nuclear vs. cytoplasmic localization patterns of transcripts 
between nodule cell type populations and between genes. Marker genes expressed in a 
specific cell type and genes expressed in a non-cell-type-specific manner but expressed 
in sufficient quantities were used for our comparative analysis. Only cells with a clearly 
defined nucleus and segmented correctly were considered. To remove ambient tran -
scriptional noise, a threshold for gene expression quantities was applied. Besides, genes 
with less than one transcript per cell and/or expressed in less than 10% of a cell type 
population were not considered in our analysis. The remaining genes had their nuclear 
densities [number of nuclear transcripts/nuclei volume (i.e., voxel)] and cytoplasmic 
densities of transcripts [number of cytoplasmic transcripts/cytoplasm volume (i.e., 
voxel)] calculated for each cell. These values were divided and log-scaled  [log10(nuclear 
density/cytoplasmic density] to calculate a ratio between nuclear localization and cyto -
plasmic localization, with higher and lower values representing increased transcript 
density in the nucleus or the cytoplasm, respectively. Pairwise Welch t-tests were con -
ducted to assess if different genes were expressed at statistically different ratios. A con -
servative Benjamini-Yekutieli procedure was then applied to these resulting p-values to 
control false positives.
Cytoplasmic distribution of transcripts in nodule‑infected cells
For each cell-gene pair, we considered the set of spatial coordinates of each transcript in 
the cytoplasm (Additional file 6: Fig. S4A). We then estimated the underlying spatial dis-
tribution via various Kernel Density Estimators (KDEs) with Gaussian kernels and band-
widths values of 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 (Additional file 6: Fig. S4B,C). These KDEs can be 
thought of as heatmaps or smoothed histograms, where higher values in the KDE cor -
respond to areas with higher transcriptomic density. We chose Gaussian kernels as they 
are the standard choice in FISH-image analysis software [68]. The values outside of the 
cell’s cytoplasm were set to 0, and the remaining values were rescaled so that the total 
KDE volume equaled 1 to maintain a valid probability density function. The KDEs were 
then rescaled by their ratio of cytoplasmic transcripts in the cell-gene pair to the total 
number of transcripts to compare the topology of the KDE for different cell-gene pairs.
Page 18 of 21Tennant et al. Genome Biology            (2026) 27:9 
Persistent homology analysis
We then performed a persistent homology analysis with a sublevel set filtration to char -
acterize key morphological features of these KDEs as a proxy to characterize the under -
lying spatial distribution of cytoplasmic transcripts. This analysis defines a nested series 
of binary images, where pixels are light-colored if they have a KDE value smaller or equal 
to the indicated level, and are dark colored otherwise (Additional file 6: Fig. S4D). The 
emergence and death of these events were recorded in a death- versus birth-level plot 
known as a persistent diagram (Additional file 6: Fig. S4E), or equivalently, in a lifetime 
versus birth plot known as a lifetime diagram (Additional file  6: Fig. S4F). In general, 
topological features with a long lifespan—persistent features—encode important global 
shape information, while those features with a shorter lifespan encode subtler geometri -
cal information [69]. The lifetime diagrams were then transformed into persistence sur -
faces to separate weighted heatmaps for each topological dimension, where each point 
is weighted according to its lifetime (Additional file  6: Fig. S4G). These heatmaps are 
discretized as n × n-pixel persistence images (Additional file 6: Fig. S4H). These images 
are flattened, and the pixel values are concatenated into a large  2n2-dimensional vec -
tor which effectively summarizes the shape information of our initial KDE, and subse -
quently the underlying spatial distribution of transcripts, in a format amenable to most 
machine learning pipelines [70]. Therefore, we computed persistence surfaces with a 
symmetric Gaussian kernel with σ = 1, a linear weighting function based on lifetime, 
and then discretized them as persistence images of 162, 242, 322, 402, and 482 pixels. 
Acknowledging that the initial transcript coordinate data were collected in the three 
dimensions of the soybean nodule cross-section resulted in 3D KDEs, and  H1, and  H2 
persistence images. Principal Component Analysis was applied to these resulting per -
sistent images and subsequent analyses focused on PC1 and PC2. Spearman correlation 
indices were computed between these PC values and the rest of spatial descriptors.
