Global burden of noncommunicable 
diseases attributable to modifiable behavioral 
risks among adolescents and young adults aged 
10–24 years, 1990–2021
Yunfei Feng1†, Da Sun2†, Xuren Sun3†, Qiqiang Guo4*, Jing Zhang5* and Yongze Li1* 
Abstract 
Background Modifiable behavioral risks are key drivers of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) in adolescents, 
yet their global burden and evolving trends remain underquantified. This study provides the first comprehensive 
assessment of NCD-related mortality and disability attributable to behavioral risks among adolescents aged 10–
24 years across countries and regions over three decades.
Methods Using data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, we estimated age-standardized disability-
adjusted life years (DALYs), mortality, and summary exposure values (SEV) for NCDs attributable to drug use, alcohol 
use, childhood sexual abuse and bullying, unsafe sex, child and maternal malnutrition, tobacco use, and intimate part-
ner violence. Trends were analyzed via estimated annual percentage changes (EAPC) with 95% confidence intervals 
(CI).
Results In 2021, behavioral risk factors contributed to 23,280 (95% uncertainty intervals [UI]: 20,960–25,530) deaths 
and 9.0 million (95% UI: 6.3–12.6) DALYs globally. Substance use disorders (4.4 million [95% UI: 3.3–5.6]) and men-
tal disorders (3.9 million [95% UI: 1.9–6.9]) were the leading contributors to DALYs, while substance use disorders 
accounted for most deaths (13,660 [95% UI: 12,880–14,500]). Between 1990 and 2021, DALYs attributable to childhood 
sexual abuse and bullying and intimate partner violence increased, whereas those related to alcohol use declined 
despite a persistent burden (2.0 million DALYs in 2021). The global SEV for tobacco showed the sharpest decline 
(− 26.5%), while DALYs for drug use rose in high-SDI regions (EAPC + 3.4%). Regional disparities were pronounced, 
with high-income North America experiencing the heaviest burden with a DALY rate of 2140 (95% UI: 1650–2680) 
per 100,000, while Latin America and the Caribbean saw high alcohol use among males and drug use among females. 
In low- and middle-income regions, increasing trends in alcohol use and childhood sexual abuse were observed.

Conclusions The burden of NCDs from behavioral risk factors in adolescents is substantial, with significant regional 
and gender disparities. Early interventions targeting alcohol use, drug use, and childhood abuse are critical, particu-
larly in high-risk regions. Tailored public health policies and further research on additional risks, like diet and physical 
inactivity, are needed to address adolescent health comprehensively.
Keywords Noncommunicable diseases, Adolescents, Sexual abuse, Bullying, Alcohol use, Drug use, Mental disorders, 
Substance use disorders
Background
Behavioral risk factors have garnered increasing atten -
tion due to their strong association with adolescent 
morbidity and mortality [1 – 4]. These risk factors not 
only impact immediate health outcomes but also con -
tribute to the development of noncommunicable dis -
eases (NCDs) in later life. For instance, in the USA, risk 
behaviors that often originate during adolescence are 
strongly linked to cardiovascular diseases and cancer, 
which together account for 59% of deaths among adults 
aged 25 years and older [3 ]. Data from the national 
Youth Risk Behavior Survey (YRBS) [5 ] indicate that 
behaviors such as smoking and alcohol consumption 
are prevalent among adolescents, highlighting the need 
for early intervention to mitigate the long-term burden 
of NCDs.
Despite growing interest, research on behavioral risks 
in adolescents often remains limited in scope [6]. A sig -
nificant limitation is the restrictive focus on narrow age 
ranges, such as university students (typically 17–23 years) 
[7] or younger adolescents (13–17 years) [8], which fails 
to capture the full spectrum of behavioral risks across the 
entire adolescent period, now widely recognized to span 
from 10 to 24 years of age based on contemporary sci -
entific consensus [9]. This expanded definition reflects 
continued brain development, particularly in prefron -
tal regions governing risk evaluation and impulse con -
trol, alongside delayed sociological transitions into adult 
roles [10]. Such a narrow focus overlooks these critical 
neurodevelopmental and social transitions that extend 
through late adolescence and early adulthood. Second, 
existing research is often geographically restricted [11], 
with studies concentrated in high-income countries or 
specific regions, such as the Americas or Europe. This 
limits the generalizability of findings to low- and middle-
income countries, where the burden of NCDs and behav-
ioral risks may be disproportionately high. Third, there 
is a lack of comprehensive data on the global burden of 
NCDs attributable to behavioral risks in adolescents. 
While some studies have examined individual risk factors 
[12–14], such as alcohol use or intimate partner violence, 
a comprehensive assessment quantifying the burden of 
multiple behavioral risks on NCDs across regions and 
demographic groups remains lacking.
To fill the gap, we analyzed data from the Global Bur -
den of Disease Database (GBD) to assess the burden of 
NCDs attributable to behavioral risk factors among ado -
lescents aged 10–24 years from 1990 to 2021. Our aims 
were to quantify DALYs and deaths associated with these 
risks, identify the leading contributing factors, and exam-
ine regional disparities. This study provides a detailed 
and comprehensive perspective on an often-overlooked 
demographic, contributing critical evidence to inform 
targeted interventions and global health policies.
Methods
Study population and data collection
This study analyzed data from the GBD 2021, obtained 
via the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) [15]. The 
GBD dataset includes the burden of 369 diseases and 
injuries across 204 countries and territories from 1990 
to 2021. We focused on adolescents aged 10–24 years 
[16] and examined NCDs attributable to behavioral risk 
factors.
Data were stratified by sex, three age groups (10–14 
years, 15–19 years, and 20–24 years), GBD regions with 
similar epidemiological profiles, sociodemographic index 
(SDI) levels, and 204 countries and territories. The data 
collection processes used in the GBD study are highly 
standardized, allowing for consistent estimates of total 
deaths and DALYs attributable to behavioral risk fac -
tors. As this study utilized publicly available, de-identi -
fied data, no ethical approval or informed consent was 
required, consistent with prior studies [17].
Definitions in GBD 2021
The GBD categorizes all risk factors into a four-level 
hierarchy and an overarching aggregate of all risk factors 
combined. Level 1 risk factors are classified as environ -
mental, occupational, behavioral, and metabolic risks. 
Behavioral risk factors include child and maternal mal -
nutrition, tobacco, high alcohol use, drug use, intimate 
partner violence, childhood sexual abuse and bullying, 
unsafe sex, dietary risks, and low physical activity [18]. 
More detailed information on the data sources for these 
risk factors is available online at GBD 2021 (https:// ghdx.

global burden attributable to the first seven behavioral 
risk factors due to the scarcity of data on dietary risks 
and low physical activity among individuals aged 10–24.
The causes of death and DALYs were categorized into 
four levels, which are mutually exclusive and collectively 
exhaustive. Level 1 included causes of death and DALYs 
from communicable, maternal, and neonatal conditions 
and nutritional diseases, NCDs, and injuries. At level 2, 
NCDs were further divided into 11 subcategories, such 
as substance use disorders. Level 3 represented more 
detailed causes within the level 2 categories, for example, 
drug use disorders within substance use disorders. Level 
4 included sub-causes of certain level 3 categories, such 
as opioid use disorders within drug use disorders.
GBD regions, and in turn GBD super-regions, are made 
up of countries and territories that are geographically 
close, epidemiologically similar, and share similar dis -
tributions of causes of death. Sociodemographic index 
(SDI) is a composite measure of lagdistributed income 
per capita, average years of education for those aged 15 
years or older, and fertility rates among females younger 
than 25 years. SDI scores were rescaled from 0 (lowest 
income and years of schooling, and highest fertility) to 
100 (highest income and years of schooling, and lowest 
fertility). The 204 countries and regions are categorized 
into five regions based on their SDI scores: low SDI, low-
middle SDI, middle SDI, high-middle SDI, and high SDI 
[19].
Statistical analysis
The disease burden attributable to behavioral risk fac -
tors was assessed using age-standardized DALY rates, 
age-standardized mortality rates, and their percentage 
changes, along with 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) for all 
estimates. Age-standardization adjusts rates to a global 
age structure, enabling valid comparisons across popula -
tions and time periods.
Estimated annual percentage changes (EAPC) and their 
95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to sum -
marize temporal trends. A log-linear regression model 
was fitted: ln(rate) = α + β × year + ε, where the regression 
coefficient β represents the annual change. The EAPC 
was calculated as 100 × (exp(β) − 1). An EAPC with a 95% 
CI excluding zero was considered statistically significant 
[19, 20].
Summary exposure values (SEV), which aggregate 
exposure data across populations while adjusting for 
demographic factors such as age and sex, were also ana -
lyzed [20]. To explore associations between SDI and dis -
ease burden, we conducted correlation analyses across 
regions and years. A 95% UI not overlapping zero was 
considered statistically significant. In this study, 95% 
UIs were derived from 1000 draws of the GBD modeling 
framework to capture data and model uncertainty, while 
95% CIs were used specifically for regression-based 
parameters such as EAPC [19]. All analyses were con -
ducted using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0) and RStudio 
(version 4.4.2), following established practices for GBD 
data analysis.
Results
Global NCD burden and trends attributable to behavioral 
risk factors
The behavioral risk factors among adolescents aged 10–
24 years globally contributed to 9.0 million (95% UI: 6.3–
12.6) DALYs and 23,280 (95% UI: 20,960–25,530) deaths 
in 2021 (Additional file 1: Table S1 and Fig. 1).
The leading contributors to DALYs were substance 
use disorders (4.4 million [95% UI: 3.3–5.6]) and mental 
disorders (3.9 million [95% UI: 1.9–6.9]). The primary 
causes of deaths were substance use disorders (13,660 
[95% UI: 12,880–14,500]).
Drug use, alcohol use, and childhood sexual abuse and 
bullying were the leading behavioral risk factors con -
tributing to DALYs. From 1990 to 2021, both the num -
ber and rate of DALYs attributable to childhood sexual 
abuse and bullying, as well as intimate partner violence, 
increased. For drug use and unsafe sex, while the number 
of DALYs rose, the rate showed a decline. Although both 
the number and rate of DALYs associated with alcohol 
use decreased over this period, the burden remains sub -
stantial, with 2.0 million DALYs (95% UI: 1.4–2.7) (Fig. 1). 
Substance use disorders and digestive diseases collec -
tively accounted for nearly 84.7% of deaths from level 2 
causes. At the level-2 cause analysis, neoplasms exhibited 
the largest increase in DALYs attributable to drug use 
(EAPC, 3.33; 95% CI: 1.96–4.77), an estimate identical to 
that for liver cancer. The main level 3 causes were drug 
use disorders, cirrhosis and other chronic liver diseases, 
and alcohol use disorders (Additional file  1: Table  S2). 
Notably, with increasing SDI, SEVs for tobacco, alcohol 
use, and child and maternal malnutrition all declined, 
with tobacco showing the largest decline at 26.5% (95% 
UI: 25.6–27.1) (Fig. 2 and Additional file 1: Fig. S1).
Sex differences in global burden and trends attributable 
to detailed behavioral risk factors
Globally, behavioral risk factors accounted for 10.5% 
(95% UI: 9.0–12.1) of male DALYs and 7.6% (95% UI: 5.9–
9.5) of female DALYs. Males had higher number and rates 
of DALYs than females (Fig. 3). However, a distinct trend 
was observed in high income, where drug use ranked as 
the leading behavioral risk factors for males, and drug 
use and childhood sexual abuse and bullying topped the 
list for attributable DALYs for females (Fig.  4). Notably, 
there was a significant fluctuation in DALYs among males

bullying during 1990–2021, with increases of 20.9% and

Regional differences in the global burden and trends 
attributable to detailed behavioral risk factors
High-income North America had the heaviest burden, at 
over twice the rate seen in Australasia. The USA, India, 
and China were the top contributors to the global bur -
den, with the USA’s DALY rate nearly 1.8 times that of 
China. The rates were 1.4 million (95% UI: 1.1–1.8) and 
0.8 million (95% UI: 0.5–1.1) per 100,000, respectively 
(Fig.  5). Geographic trends revealed sharp contrasts in 
EAPC (Additional file 1: Table S3). Worldwide, the EAPC 
was negatively correlated to the relative DALYs (R = 0.57, 
P < 1 ×  10−4) and SDI in 2021 (R = 0.93, P < 1 ×  10−13). 
Notably, drug use and childhood sexual abuse and 
bullying emerged as consistent leading risks across sexes 
and regions, with drug use predominantly driving DALYs 
in high-income region. In Latin America and Carib -
bean, the DALY burden was more significantly associated 
with high alcohol use for males and drug use for females 
(Fig. 4). The detailed burden data (deaths and DALYs) for 
each behavioral risk factor, broken down by GBD region 
and SDI quintile for the years 1990 and 2021, are pro -
vided in Additional file 1: Tables S4–S8.

regional factors significantly influenced the distribution 
and trends of the global burden of NCDs attributable 
to behavioral risk factors. Our study extends previous 
research [6, 7] by providing a comprehensive analysis of 
key behavioral risk factors: childhood sexual abuse and 
bullying, drug use, alcohol use, unsafe sex, child and

increased internet usage. Additionally, in 76.2% of GBD 
regions, the impact of childhood sexual abuse and bul -
lying is most pronounced in females, with Australasia 
exhibiting the highest rates. Moreover, SEV trends for 
childhood sexual abuse and bullying rose in lower- and

Drug use
In the case of drug use, we observed significant regional 
differences. The USA, India, China, and Brazil contrib -
uted the most to the global drug use burden, with the 
USA showing the highest DALY burden at 1.3 million 
(95% UI: 1.0–1.5) (Additional file 1: Table S6). Drug use 
was the leading risk factor for males in 42.9% of regions, 
with particularly high burdens in Eastern Europe and 
high-income Asia Pacific. In high-income North Amer -
ica, this phenomenon is only observed in females. In 
high-income regions, drug use accounted for more than 
half of the excess male DALYs, reflecting greater availa -
bility of psychoactive substances, social normalization of 
recreational use, and inadequate early prevention efforts. 
By contrast, in South Asia and Latin America, male vul -
nerability to drug initiation may be influenced by prevail-
ing gender norms and peer influences [24, 25].
Studies [26, 27] have reported that the earlier the use 
of psychoactive drugs, the greater the lifelong risk of 
drug use disorders. Therefore, it is crucial to immedi -
ately interrupt adolescents’ exposure to drugs to mini -
mize harm. Targeted interventions should address these

widespread concerns about alcohol damage and govern -

decline in alcohol-related DALY rates in many high-SDI 
regions may partly reflect the impact of policy interven -

Reduce the Harmful Use of Alcohol (2010), minimum 
legal drinking age laws, increased taxation, and restric -

reduce alcohol availability and harmful use among ado -
lescents [32]. Evidence from Europe and North America 
indicates that stronger policy enforcement correlates 
with reduced alcohol-attributable mortality and morbid -
ity in youth populations, for example, between 2010 and 
2016 the proportion of all deaths attributable to alco -
hol among Europeans aged 20–24 years fell from 30.4% 
to 23.3%, while alcohol-related DALY rates in the same 
age group declined by 25% [33], which may explain the 
downward burden trends identified in our study.

Intimate partner violence exhibited the highest bur -

absolute numbers for intimate partner violence were 
lower compared to other risk factors, it remains a sig -
nificant concern for adolescent health. Importantly, ado -
lescents and young people aged 15–24 are not exempt 
from intimate partner violence. Global survey data show 
that nearly one in four adolescent girls aged 15–19 years 
who have ever been in a relationship report experienc -

This highlights that intimate partner violence is not solely 
an adult phenomenon but also a pressing adolescent and 
young adult health issue. The gender-specific patterns in 
the burden attributable to distinct behavioral risk fac -
tors can be explained by multiple determinants. Males 
are more likely to engage in high-risk behaviors such as 
tobacco, alcohol, and drug use, partly due to sociocul -
tural norms, peer influences, and greater risk-taking 
tendencies. In contrast, females are disproportionately 
affected by intimate partner violence and childhood sex -
ual abuse, which are linked to lower access to education, 
limited social support, and structural gender inequalities 
in many regions [36–38]. Biological factors, including 
hormonal and developmental differences, may further 
exacerbate these disparities. These findings underscore 
the importance of tailoring interventions to gender-spe -
cific vulnerabilities.
Strengths and limitations
Our study adopted the updated, globally comprehensive 
data from the GBD 2021, which allowed for a detailed 
analysis of the burden of NCDs attributable to behavioral 
risk factors in adolescents aged 10–24. This large-scale, 
population-based approach provides robust estimates of 
the global and regional burden, enabling the identifica -
tion of significant patterns and trends across gender, age, 
and geographic regions. The inclusion of a wide range 
of countries and territories from different SDI regions 
strengthens the generalizability of our findings.
However, our study also has limitations. During the 
GBD 2021 data retrieval, no usable or disaggregated 
estimates were available for dietary risks and low physi -
cal activity in the adolescent population, making it infea -
sible to include them in our comparative analysis. This 
limitation may have resulted in an incomplete assess -
ment of the full spectrum of adolescent behavioral risks. 
Second, our estimates rely on the GBD 2021 modeling 
framework, which uses multiple data sources, impu -
tation, and proxy data in regions with limited surveil -
lance capacity. While this standardized approach allows 
for global comparability, it may lead to uncertainty in 
estimates, particularly for low-income and conflict-
affected settings where empirical data are scarce. There -
fore, results should be interpreted as modeled estimates 
rather than direct observations. Furthermore, while we 
focused on five key behavioral risk factors, other factors, 
such as environmental risks and mental health condi -
tions, could also play significant roles in the NCD bur -
den and deserve further exploration. To enrich this field 
of research, future studies should explore the impact of 
other behavioral risk factors, such as physical inactiv -
ity and poor diet, on adolescent health outcomes. Given 
the rising rates of obesity and sedentary behavior glob -
ally, these factors may become more prominent in future 
NCD burdens. Additionally, further research is needed 
to understand the mechanisms driving the regional and 
gender disparities observed in our study. Future work 
should also aim to improve data collection and modeling 
techniques to address the gaps in regions with limited or 
poor-quality data, particularly in low-income countries.
This study provides a comprehensive, global perspec -
tive on the adolescent NCD burden attributable to behav-
ioral risk factors, an area that has been underexplored in 
previous research. By focusing on adolescents aged 10–
24, we highlight the critical importance of early interven -
tion and prevention efforts to reduce the future burden 
of NCDs. The findings underscore the need for targeted 
public health policies and interventions that address the 
specific behavioral risks faced by adolescents, particularly 
in high-risk regions and vulnerable populations.
Conclusions
This study found that behavioral risk factors, especially 
childhood sexual abuse and bullying, drug use, and alco -
hol use, are significant contributors to the global burden 
of NCDs in adolescents. The findings highlight the need 
for targeted interventions and policies that focus on these 
risks, with a particular emphasis on addressing gender 
and regional disparities. Our research adds valuable evi -
dence to the ongoing discussions about adolescent health 
and the urgent need for preventive measures to reduce 
the future burden of NCDs.
