BMC Medicine
The brief resilience scale: a genome-wide 
association study in the UK Biobank
Marilyn C. Cornelis1*, John A. Caldwell2, Thanh Huyen T. Vu1, Stephen R. Hennigar3, Claire E. Berryman3 and 
Harris R. Lieberman2 
Abstract 
Background Some individuals exposed to traumatic stressors develop psychiatric disorders while others remain 
resilient. The Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) assesses the ability to “bounce back” from stress and is a widely used measure 
of trait resilience. We performed the first genome-wide association study of BRS in the UK Biobank (UKB).
Methods Beginning 2022, a subset of UKB participants completed an on-line mental-health questionnaire 
that included the six-item BRS. BRS data and genome-wide typing and imputation were available for 124,774 par-
ticipants of European ancestry. Genome-wide linear tests of BRS were performed, followed by SNP-based heritability 
and cross-trait genetic correlation analyses. Nominally significant loci (P < 5 ×  10−6 ) were followed up for candidate 
gene mapping.
Results SNP-based heritability of BRS was 7.3% and strong genetic correlations (rg) were observed with neuroti-
cism (rg, − 0.70 to − 0.44), depression (rg, − 0.63 to − 0.37) and anxiety (rg, − 0.81 to − 0.46). Three loci met genome-wide 
significance (P < 5 ×  10−8 , near VRK2, TNKS/MSRA and RAB36) and 29 loci met nominal significance (P < 5 ×  10−6 ). None 
of these were replicated in prior GWAS using different measures of resilience. The strongest candidate genes prior-
itized on the basis of both functional and biological evidence include VRK2 (2p16.1) (previously associated with neu-
ropsychiatric disorders) and MSRA (8p23.1) (reduces methionine sulfoxide to methionine). Others at nominally signifi-
cant loci include SLC6A9 (1p34.1) (encodes a glycine transporter), NPY (7p15.2) (involved in stress response), CADPS2 
(7q31.32) (involved in synaptic vesicle exocytosis), and PCDH9 (13q21.32) (involved in neural tissue cell adhesion). 
Conclusions Our findings provide further support for a genetic basis to trait resilience and one shared with psychiat-
ric disorders and personality traits including depression, anxiety, and neuroticism. Our promising loci warrant replica-
tion but offer new biological insight to resilience. 
Keywords Genetics, Resilience, UK biobank, Psychiatric disorders, Personality
Background
Individuals exposed to traumatic stressors are at 
increased risk for psychiatric disorders such as post -
traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and major depressive 
disorder (MDD). However, some exposed individuals 
are resilient and do not develop such disorders. Defini -
tions of psychological resilience vary [1]. The American 
Psychological Association defines resilience as “the pro -
cess and outcome of successfully adapting to difficult or 
challenging life experiences, especially through mental, 
emotional, and behavioral flexibility and adjustment to

external and internal demands” [2]. Assets and resources 
within the individual, the events they encounter in life, 
and the characteristics of their environment may facili -
tate adaptation and “bouncing back” in the face of adver-
sity, but whether resilience is a dynamic state, a mental/
intellectual, social process, or a stable trait is unclear [1]. 
Resilience research often focuses on the influence of 
environmental and social factors [3], but twin studies 
suggest genetics account for up to 52% of the observed 
variance in resilience [4–6]. The precise genetic variants 
remain largely unknown. Several studies have focused 
on variation in candidate genes such as SLC6A4, BDNF, 
COMT, DRD4, OXTR, and NPY  [7, 8]. Genome-wide 
association studies (GWAS) largely sample vulnerable 
populations and have focused on the absence of psycho -
pathology following stress or trauma [7]. To our knowl -
edge, only two GWAS have focused on self-reported 
trait resilience. Stein et  al. [9] conducted a GWAS of 
self-assessed resilience among 11,492 European ancestry 
soldiers in the US Army Study to Assess Risk and Resil -
ience in Service members (STARRS) and reported asso -
ciations with variants near DCLK2. KLHL36 was also 
identified in gene-based testing. Herrera-Rivero et  al. 
[10] more recently performed a GWAS of six German 
cohorts (N = 15,822) who had completed the 11-item 
Resilience Scale (RS-11). No variants met genome-wide 
significance, but gene-based analysis identified three 
genes (ROBO1, CIB3, and LYPD4 ). The STARRS 5-item 
resilience questionnaire included the following: (i) keep 
calm and think of the right thing to do in a crisis; (ii) 
manage stress; (iii) try new approaches if old ones do not 
work; (iv) get along with people when you have to; and 
(v) keep your sense of humor in tense situations. RS-11 
items assessed self-esteem, optimism and internal locus 
of control. Both instruments possess good internal con -
sistency, convergent validity, and predictive validity, but 
none have been validated or well-studied outside of the 
military (STARRS 5-item) or German (RS-11) population 
[11–13].
The Brief Resilience Scale (BRS) is a 6-item meas -
ure designed to assess the ability to “bounce back” from 
stress [14]. BRS has been translated into at least six lan -
guages, and validation studies report good psychometric 
properties to include high internal consistency and test/
retest-reliability [14–21]. BRS correlates with other resil -
ience measures (i.e., CD-RISC) [14], personal character -
istics (optimism, neuroticism, personality type) [14, 20, 
22], social relationships [14], coping styles [20, 23], and 
mental health outcomes [14, 20, 23]. Compared to other 
resilience scales, the psychometric properties of the BRS 
rate high on internal consistency, convergent validity, and 
discriminant predictive validity [15].
In the current study, we perform the first GWAS of BRS 
and nominate several candidate loci based on functional 
relevance and biological plausibility. We also provide fur-
ther evidence for shared genetics between resilience and 
other psychological traits and disorders.
Methods
UK Biobank (UKB)
From 2006 to 2010 (baseline), the UKB recruited  over 
502,633 participants aged 37–73 years at 22 centers 
across England, Wales, and Scotland [24]. Participants 
provided informed written consent before completing 
touchscreen questionnaires, an interviewer-administered 
questionnaire, a physical assessment, and biospecimen 
collection [25]. Subsets of the cohort completed addi -
tional in-person follow-ups and online questionnaires. 
The present study was covered by the generic ethical 
approval for UKB studies from the National Research 
Ethics Service Committee North West–Haydock 
(approval letter dated 17th June 2011, Ref 11/NW/0382). 
All study procedures were performed in accordance with 
the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki 
ethical principles for medical research.
Mental health questionnaire and brief resilience scale (BRS)
UKB participants with valid emails completed on-line 
mental-health questionnaires on two occasions—first 
beginning in 2016 and next beginning in 2022. BRS was 
part of the second of these two mental health question -
naires. At the time of the current analysis, this question -
naire had been completed by up to 175,236 participants. 
The BRS consists of six questions (Table  1) answered 
on a five-point scale from “Strongly Disagree” (1) to 
“Strongly Agree” (5). Odd-numbered questions are posi -
tively worded and even-numbered questions are nega -
tively worded. Even-numbered questions are reverse 
scored, and a total resilience score consists of the mean 
of the six items and can range from 1 to 5 with higher 
numbers indicating higher resilience. We computed BRS 
for participants who completed all six items for a total 
of 169,319 participants. We used other data collected 
on this questionnaire for descriptive purposes including 
measures of self-reported well-being, loneliness, lifetime 
adverse events, current depressive symptoms (patient 
health questionnaire, PHQ-9), current general anxiety 
disorder (Generalized Anxiety Disorder [GAD]7) and 
current alcohol consumption and alcohol-related behav -
iors and problems (Alcohol Use Disorder Identification 
Tool-AUDIT) (Additional file 1: Table S1) [26–38].

derived (field 20127) using the short form of the Eysenck 
Personality Questionnaire-Revised. The scale included

Genetic data
All UKB participants were genotyped using genome-
wide arrays [40]. Quality control and imputation to the 
HRC v1.1 and 1000G + UK10K reference panels were 
performed centrally by the Wellcome Trust Centre for 
Human Genetics [40]. We excluded sample outliers 
based on heterozygosity and missingness, participants 
with sex discrepancies between the self-reported and 
X-chromosome heterozygosity, and those potentially 
related to other participants based on estimated kinship 
coefficients for all pairs of samples. We considered analy-
sis of participants of European (EUR, ~ 96% of sample), 
African (AFR, ~ 1%), Central/South Asian (CSA, ~ 2%) 
and East Asian (EAS, ~ 0.6%) genetically inferred ances -
try based on a principal component analysis (PCA) by 
Pan-UKB[40, 41].
Analytic sample
Our primary genetic analysis included all UKB partici -
pants with a BRS score (initially 169,319) and who were 
also unrelated and of EUR ancestry with quality genetic 
data, leaving 124,774. A subsample self-identifying as 
British (N = 118,827) were examined in sensitivity analy -
sis. UKB samples of AFR (N = 823), CSA (N = 1177) and 
EAS (N = 598) ancestry were too small for meaningful 
replication.
Statistical and Bioinformatic Analysis
All individual-level statistical analyses were performed 
using the SAS statistical package (version 9.1 for UNIX; 
SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and PLINK2 [42, 43]. We 
assessed internal consistency of BRS using Cronbach’s 
alpha and the factor structure of the scale by PCA with 
a varimax rotation retaining eigenvalues > 1 [44]. Conver-
gent validity of BRS was evaluated by computing Pear -
son (r) correlations with measures of depression, anxiety, 
alcohol consumption (AUDIT-C), alcohol problematic 
use (AUDIT-P), well-being, adverse life events, and neu -
roticism (Additional file 1: Table S1) [26–38].
BRS GWAS
We performed genome-wide (GW) linear regressions 
assuming an additive genetic model and adjusting for age, 
sex, and top 10 EUR-specific PCs. Only di-allelic SNPs 
with MAF ≥ 5% and imputation quality scores > 0.4 were 
retained in the final summary statistics for GW plots 
and subsequent analyses and replications (n = 6,125,901). 
FUMA was used for displaying, clumping, and anno -
tating summary-level results for SNPs with dbSNP ids 
(n = 6,122,886)[45]. Functional enrichment was per -
formed with ANNOVAR [46] and stratified-LDSC [47]; 
each uses different annotations and statistical algorithms 
(Additional file  1: Supplementary Methods). SNPs were 
annotated to functional annotation databases including 
CADD [48] and RegulomeDB [49]. Genome-wide signifi -
cance was defined as P < 5 ×  10−8, but all lead SNP-BRS 
associations at P < 5 ×  10−6 were taken forward for fine-
mapping and additional bioinformatic characterization. 
Lead SNPs were defined as P < 5 ×  10 −6 and independ -
ent of each other (r 2 < 0.1). A minimum r2 of 0.8 defined 
independent significant SNPs which determined the bor -
ders of each genomic risk locus. We identified potentially 
independent lead variants by re-running regressions con-
ditioning on the lead SNP from each locus. We tested for

Fine‑mapping
All nominally significant loci (P < 5 ×  10−6) were subject to 
functionally informed fine mapping using PolyFun incor -
porating FINEMAP [52]. The method generates SNP-
wise posterior inclusion probabilities of causality (PIP), 
and a 95% credible set, defined as the minimum subset 
of SNPs that cumulatively have at least 95% probability 
of containing the causal SNP(s). We applied the stand -
ard PolyFun workflow, integrating prior causal prob -
abilities based on per-SNP heritability and summary LD 
information pre-calculated using individuals of European 
ancestry from UKB [47]. We used functional annotations 
from the baseline-LF2.2.UKB model [53] capturing epi -
genomic and genomic annotations, MAF bins, binary or 
continuous functional annotations, LD-related annota -
tions such as LD level, predicted allele age, recombina -
tion rate, and CpG content [53]. The MHC region (chr6 
25.5–33.5 M) and two other long-range LD regions (chr8 
8–12 M, chr11 46–57 M) were excluded from analyses, 
since FINEMAP tends to produce spurious results in 
these regions[52]. Variants without pre-computed val -
ues were also filtered out. Fine-mapping spanned regions 
500 kb upstream and 500 kb downstream of the locus 
bounds. A maximum of 5 causal SNPs was specified for 
each locus. SNPs with PIP > 0.5 were deemed likely causal 
which we note is more liberal than the traditional thresh-
old of PIP > 0.95 [52].
Heritability and genetic correlations
We used LDSC [54] routines implemented in the Com -
plex-Traits Genetic Virtual Lab (CTG-VL) [55] to esti -
mate SNP heritability  (h2
SNP) and cross-trait genetic 
correlations (r g). For the latter, we considered GWAS 
summary data of 2871 complex traits and diseases 
and applied a significance threshold of P < 1.7 ×  10 −5 to 
account for multiple testing. Considering results, we 
conducted post hoc genetic correlations using summary 
statistics from a larger GWAS of neuroticism and three 
subdomains (“depression affect, ” “worry, ” “sensitivity to 
environmental stress and adversity”) as well as bidirec -
tional Mendelian Randomization (MR) analyses of MDD 
and anxiety disorder (AD) (Additional file 1:Supplemen -
tary Methods) [56–70].
Gene‑based, functional enrichment and pathway analyses
We performed gene-based association testing for BRS 
in FUMA [45], with MAGMA [71], using the matched-
ancestry 1000G v3 panel as LD reference. Input SNPs 
were mapped to 20,227 protein coding genes. Signifi -
cant genes were defined as P  = 0.05/20227 = 2.47 ×  10− 6. 
MAGMA gene-set analysis was performed for curated 
gene sets and GO terms obtained from MsigDB, cor -
recting for gene size, variant density, and LD within 
and between genes. We also used MAGMA to test the 
association between gene-set properties and tissue-
specific gene expression profiles using GTEx (v.7) data 
from 53 tissues. Given gene expression is a potential 
causal mechanism linking variant to phenotype, tran -
scriptome-wide association (TWA) testing was also 
performed after integrating GWAS summary statistics 
and joint expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) data 
across 12 brain tissue regions using SMultiXcan [72] 
as implemented in CTG-VL. Significant genes were 
defined as P  = 0.05/14,006 genes tested = 3.57 ×  10−6.
Replication
We requested summary statistics of the GWAS for 
self-assessed STARRS [9 ] resilience in US soldiers and 
RS-11in German civilians for replication of our top 
SNP-BRS associations. We also scanned our genome-
wide results for candidate resilience SNPs and genes 
extracted from these two resilience GWAS [9 , 10] and a 
comprehensive review [7 ].
Candidate gene selection and prioritization
Using FUMA [45], protein-coding genes were mapped 
if they were located within 10 kb of the index vari -
ants or if annotated to the gene based on cis-eQTL 
(LD ≥ 0.8) or chromatin interaction (CI, LD ≥ 0.8) data 
from human brain [73– 78]. We supplemented FUMA 
annotations with methylation QTL (mQTL, LD ≥ 0.8) 
data from human brain [79]. For loci with no candi -
date genes meeting these criteria, the closest gene was 
selected. Each gene was annotated a score for predicted 
loss of impact (pLI) [80] and non-coding residual vari -
ation intolerance score (ncRVIS) [81]. Literature per -
taining to candidate genes was manually curated. Genes 
were prioritized based on evidence across all evidence 
categories—FUMA positional, eQTL, mQTL and chro -
matin interaction mapping; variant and gene annota -
tion scores (PIP > 0.5, CADD > 12.37, RegulomeDB < 2, 
pLI > 0.9, ncRVIS > 0); significance in gene-based analy -
ses and TWA; and biological plausibility.

Results
PCA of EUR-BRS revealed a one-factor solution account-
ing for 62% of the variance (Table  1). Loadings ranged 
from 0.66 to 0.84. Internal consistency was good: Cron -
bach’s alpha was 0.88. Mean (SD) BRS for EUR was 3.61 
(0.74). Only 5% and 0.3% of EUR achieved the highest 
and lowest score suggesting minimal ceiling/floor effects. 
Results were similar when extended to all 169,319 UKB 
participants with BRS (Additional file  1: Table  S2). Of 
note, mean (SD) BRS scores for AFR, CSA, and EAS 
subsamples were 3.79 (0.76), 3.65 (0.74), and 3.60 (0.76), 
respectively. BRS was significantly (P < 0.0001) and 
strongly correlated with PHQ9 (r = − 0.44), GAD7 (r = 
− 0.45), happiness (or “positive affect” , r = 0.47), mean -
ingfulness (r = 0.39), and neuroticism (r = − 0.47) in 
directions expected (Additional file  1: Table  S3). Base -
line (2006–2010) characteristics of the UKB-EUR sam -
ple for GWAS alongside those of the full UKB cohort are 
presented in Table  2. Mean (SD) age at the time of BRS 
measurement was 69.9 (7.5) years.
BRS GWAS
Three independent SNPs associated with BRS at genome-
wide significance (P < 5 ×  10−8, Table  3, Additional file 1: 
Fig S1). Although our λGC was 1.19, the intercept was 1.03 
(SE = 0.01) and attenuation ratio 0.12 (SE = 0.05), indi -
cating that ~ 88% of the observed inflation in test statis -
tics was due to a polygenic signal [54] (Additional file 1: 
Fig. S2). We identified a total of 1660 candidate SNPs 
(P < 5 ×  10−6, independent SNPs as well as those in LD 
with them at r 2 ≥ 0.8), and 38 lead SNPs (P < 5 ×  10−6 and 
independent of one another at LD r2 < 0.1) (Table 3, Addi-
tional file 1: Fig. S3, Additional file 2: Table S4). Sex strati-
fied analyses revealed significant heterogeneity (P < 0.05) 
for three of these loci: the lead SNP at 1q42.12 (rs708767) 
was more strongly associated with BRS among males (β = 
− 0.02, P = 3.5 ×  10−7) than females (β = −0.01, P = 0.006) 
while lead SNPs rs13089093 (3p13) and rs10057995 
(5q14.3) were significant among females (P = 2.0 ×  10−7 
and P = 2.0 ×  10−8, respectively) but not men (P = 0.25 
and P = 0.34, respectively). Conditional analysis did not 
yield additional independent loci. Candidate SNPs were 
enriched in intronic (45%, P = 1.5 ×  10−13), intergenic 
(37%, P = 3.6 ×  10−14), UTR3 (2%, P = 9.0 ×  10−5), and 
UTR5 (0.6%, P = 9.4 ×  10−3) regions based on ANNO -
VAR. LDSC revealed a significant and high-level enrich -
ment of heritability in conserved regions: 2.5% of the 
SNPs in conserved regions accounted for 46% of the total 
heritability (Enrichment = 18.8, P = 2.3 ×  10−5) Extending 
boundaries of these regions 500  bp also yielded signifi -
cant enrichment (Enrichment 2.5, P = 4.8 ×  10−8). SNPs 
within 500 bp of repressed regions showed a signifi -
cant reduction in the level of heritability they captured 
(Enrichment = 0.75, P = 0.001). However, these regions 
accounted for 72% of the SNPs, but only explained 54% 
of the heritability. A total of 24 SNPs (5 loci) have a 
high likelihood of a regulatory function based on Regu -
lomeDB scores < 2; 58 SNPs (16 loci) are potentially path-
ogenic based on CADD scores > 12.37 (Additional file 2: 
Table  S5). Seven likely causal variants with PIP > 0.50 
were identified (Table  3); none met the more stringent 
threshold of PIP > 0.95. Functional annotations for these 
7 SNPs are presented in Additional file 2: Table S6.

Mendelian randomization
We followed up the observed genetic correlations with 
depression and anxiety traits with two-sample inverse-
variance weighted (IVW) MR analysis of BRS-MDD and 
BRS-AD (Additional file  1: Table  S8). Twenty-two and 
20 SNPs (P < 5 ×  10−6) for BRS were selected as IVs for 
MDD and AD, respectively (Additional file 1: Fig. S4 and 
Fig. S5). The MR logs odds ratio (OR, 95% CI, P-value) 
for MDD and AD were 0.65 (0.56–0.75, P = 2.4 ×  10−9) 
and 0.59 (0.38–0.93, P = 0.02), respectively, per geneti -
cally predicted increase in BRS score. An MR using a 
weighted-median model supported the causal asso -
ciation with MDD but not with AD. Heterogeneity and 
pleiotropic effects were ruled out in sensitivity analy -
sis (Additional file  1: Table  S8, Fig. S4 and Fig. S5). In 
MRs testing whether susceptibility to AD (21 SNPs, 
P < 5 ×  10−6) is a causal factor for BRS, we observed that 
each log-OR increment in AD risk was associated with a 
0.07 (SE = 0.01) lower BRS score (P = 2.2 ×  10−10) (Addi -
tional file 1: Table S8, Fig. S6). Significant heterogeneity 
(Q-value = 0.0016) and pleiotropy effects (MR-PRESSO 
global test = 0.001) impacted corresponding results 
for MDD with IVs defined using P < 5 ×  10 −6. The lat -
ter was remedied using an MDD IV limited to 30 SNPs 
with P < 5 ×  10−8. Each log-OR increment in MDD risk 
was associated with a 0.15 (SE = 0.02) lower BRS score 
(P = 4.49 ×  10−11) (Additional file 1: Table S8, Fig. S7).
Gene-based, functional enrichment and pathway analyses
Gene-based testing yielded 7 significant protein coding 
genes (P < 2.47 ×  10−6): VRK2, FANCL, ZNF396, PCDH9, 
RTDR1, ZNF266, and RAB36 (Additional file 2: Table S9); 
all spanning promising genomic loci (Table  3). Applying 
TWA testing yielded 2 significant (P < 3.57 ×  10−6) genes: 
VRK2 and ZNF396 (Additional file  2: Table  S10). Over -
all, no significant functional or pathway enrichment was 
observed.
Replication
We compared our results to that of a GWAS for self-
assessed STARRS[9] resilience in US soldiers and RS-11 
in German civilians (Additional file  1: Table  S11). Two 
of our lead SNPs associated with RS-11: rs72706362 
(P = 0.03) and rs7306560 (P = 0.01). One lead SNP asso -
ciated with STARRS resilience: rs61957642 (P = 0.0026). 
None of these are significant after multiple testing cor -
rection. Moreover, none of the nominal SNP associations 
(P < 5 ×  10−6) reported in these previous GWAS were 
replicated (P < 0.05 and directionally consistent) with 
BRS in the current study. Genetic correlations with BRS 
were moderate: RS-11, r g = 0.49, P = 0.0002 and STARRS, 
rg = 0.43, P = 0.01. Look-ups of previously proposed resil -
ient genes in our two gene-centric analysis identified 
L3MBTL2 (P = 0.0006 and P = 0.0003).
Candidate gene selection and prioritization
Upon review of regional plots, we excluded 6 loci from 
further follow-up because they represented single vari -
ants (Table  3, Additional file  1: Fig. S3). Genome-wide 
(P < 5 ×  10−8) and nominally significant SNPs (P < 5 ×  10−6) 
within the remaining 32 loci mapped to 99 protein-cod -
ing genes under at least one mapping strategy (Additional 
file 2: Table S12, Additional file 3: Fig. S8). Twenty-three 
of these candidate genes having the most supporting evi -
dence (functional or biological) are presented in Table  4. 
Candidate genes VRK2 and TNKS at genome-wide signif-
icant loci are the focus of additional discussion.
Discussion
This is the first GWAS of BRS, a self-assessed measure of 
psychological resilience used widely in the psychosocial 
research field. We found significant genetic correlations 
between BRS and various psychological traits and disor -
ders; most notably, negative correlations with depression, 
anxiety, and neuroticism. We identified three genome-
significant loci and several other promising loci with 
strong functional and biological plausibility. Some of 
these promising loci overlapped with those identified for 
other psychiatric traits in GWAS, but in some instances, 
the directions of the effects were not always consistent 
with phenotypic correlations. Finally, we observed mini -
mal overlap between our BRS loci and those identified in 
previous GWAS [9, 10] using different measures of self-
assessed resilience.
SNP-based heritability was 7.3% for BRS. We observed 
strong and significant genetic correlations with neu -
roticism and measures of anxiety and depression that 
were consistent with phenotypic correlations. GWAS of 
self-assessed STARRS [9] resilience in US soldiers and 
RS-11 [10] in German civilians both reported negative 
genetic correlations (LDSC r g = − 0.39 and LDpred2 r = 
− 0.07, respectively) with UKB neuroticism, while the 
RS-11 study additionally reported a positive correlation 
with happiness. The larger correlation between BRS and 
neuroticism (r g = − 0.67) reported in the current study 
likely reflects bias due to the overlapping UKB samples. 
A recent meta-analysis estimated an average pheno -
typic correlation of r = − 0.46 between neuroticism and 
various resilience measures [82]. Neuroticism and BRS 
were negatively correlated (r = − 0.48) in the Generation

Scotland: Scottish Family Health Study [22]. Neuroti -
cism is a relatively stable and partly-heritable personal -
ity trait characterized by emotional instability, negative 
emotional response, and stress sensitivity [83]. Trait resil-
ience has also been referred to as a personality charac -
terized by positive emotionality [82, 84]. In the current 
study, BRS was more negatively correlated (r g) with the 
“worry” subdomain of neuroticism suggesting a latent 
factor of worry/anxiety associated with neuroticism and 
resilience scales might be driving phenotypic and genetic 
correlations. An item-level phenotypic and genetic 
investigation of resilience and neuroticism is warranted, 
preferably using independent samples. MR analysis 
supported bidirectional relationships between BRS and 
AD and BRS and MDD. In longitudinal and intervention 
studies, higher resilience scale scores are associated with 
fewer symptoms of anxiety and depression [85]. While a 
causal role of BRS is plausible, it is also possible that psy -
chiatric illness influences perceived resilience. Potential 
overlap between BRS items and criteria used to diagnose 
AD and MDD may also contribute to these bidirectional 
relationships.

intronic and UTR5 regions of VRK2 and have potentially 
pathogenic or regulatory function implications. SNP var -

expression in the brain. The same locus associates with 
several behavioral and psychiatric traits in GWAS [86, 
87] (Additional file 2: Table S13). Notably, SNP variants 
linked to higher BRS associate with favorable brain mor -
phology (greater volume/thickness/depth), decreased 
risk of depression and schizophrenia, but lower educa -
tional attainment. Educational attainment is a strong cor-
relate of cognitive ability, but is also influenced by many 
other factors, such as personality [88]. VRK2 encodes 
a member of the vaccinia-related kinase family of ser -
ine/threonine protein kinases that acts as an effector of 
signaling pathways that regulate apoptosis and tumor 
cell growth. How this function impacts resilience and 
psychiatric disorders is unclear. Recently, VRK2 blood 
expression was reportedly lower in depressed vs healthy 
participants [89]. VRK2 deficient mice exhibit depres -
sive-like behaviors vs wildtype, but there are no differ -
ences in exploratory behavior, motor ability, or sucrose 
preference [89].
Genome-wide significant SNPs for BRS span 8p23.1, 
which is  a known inversion region with high LD [90]. 
This region comprises genes related to innate immunity 
and the nervous system and is a potential hub for can -
cer and developmental neuropsychiatric disorders [91]. 
Most of our candidate genes present with more intoler -
ant noncoding regions than expected. TNKS was prior -
itized on the basis of functional mapping. Significant 
SNPs spanned the intronic, exonic, and UTR3 regions of 
this gene; some potentially pathogenic per CADD scores. 
SNP variants associated with higher BRS correlate with 
lower TNKS expression in the brain and lower neuroti -
cism, lower waist circumference, but also lower well-
being and lower educational attainment in published 
GWAS (Additional file  2: Table  S13). TNKS encodes a 
poly-ADP-ribosyltransferase involved in the Wnt sign -
aling pathway, telomere length, and vesicle trafficking 
[92–94] which have no obvious role in psychological 
resilience. SNP variants linked to higher BRS also corre -
late with lower brain expression of MSRA , another can -
didate gene that encodes methionine sulfoxide reductase 
A, which reduces methionine sulfoxide to methionine 
[95]. MsrA serves as a key repair enzyme for proteins that 
have been inactivated by oxidation, since the oxidation of 
Met residues can alter protein function and may be lethal 
for the cell [95]. MsrA may modulate catechol-O-meth -
yltransferase activity in the brain, and, thus, indirectly 
impact the dopamine system [96]. MsrA knockout mice 
have compromised complex task learning ability, locomo-
tor activity, and dopamine regulation [97, 98]. MSRA is a 
positional candidate gene for schizophrenia (rs3088186), 
neuroticism (rs6982308), math ability (rs7832708), well-
being (rs7832708), and smoking initiation (rs7838901) 
in GWAS. Specific SNPs identified in the latter did not 
reach our significance threshold of P < 5 ×  10−6 with BRS 
but were all at least P < 0.004.
The third genome-wide significant locus 22q11.23 is 
potentially functional but lacks clear biological connec -
tions to resilience.
Several nominally significant SNPs associated with 
BRS are supported by functional and biological plausi -
bility (Table  4). For example, the 1p34.1 locus maps to 
SLC6A9 encoding glycine transporter 1 (GlyT1). GlyT1 
inhibits Gly signaling by removing it from the synaptic 
cleft [99]. Gly is an inhibitory neurotransmitter in the 
CNS but can also be found in glutamatergic synapses, 
acting as a co-agonist of NMDA receptors, and there -
fore modulating excitatory neurotransmission. Impaired 
NMDA receptor functioning has been implicated in neu -
rological and psychiatric disorders [99–101]. As a result, 
numerous selective GlyT1 inhibitors have been devel -
oped to enhance Gly concentration in the synaptic cleft 
and indirectly augment the activity of NMDA receptors. 
GlyT1 inhibitors have been used in trials for the manage -
ment of schizophrenia, neuropathic pain, drug addiction, 
epilepsy, anxiety, and obsessive compulsive disorder [99]. 
Variants at this locus also correlated with red blood cell 
(RBC) traits in GWAS, consistent with a role of GlyT1 
in heme biosynthesis [102, 103]. NPY, encoding neu -
ropeptide Y, is a plausible gene mapping to the 7p15.3 
locus. The NPY system is implicated in the regulation of 
fear, anxiety, and stress and is a classic candidate gene in 
resilience research [7, 104]. The 1q42.12 locus includes 
ITPKB encoding a protein that regulates the metabo -
lism of inositol phosphates and potentially ER-to-mito -
chondria calcium release [105]. Variant effect sizes at 
this locus were larger for males than for females. ITPKB  
expression is upregulated in males compared to females 
[106]. Another variant in ITPKB  (rs4653767-T) associ -
ated with increased risk of Parkinson’s disease in GWAS 
[107] was associated with higher BRS in the current study 
(P = 0.0009 in males, P = 0.04 in females). Altered ITPKB  
expression has been implicated in Alzheimer’s disease 
pathogenesis in females but not males [108]. Given the 
sex-differential in ITPKB  expression and prevalences of 
Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease, a male-prominent 
role of ITPKB  in psychological resilience is possible 
and warrants further study. The 7q31.32 locus includes 
CADPS2 and encodes a member of the calcium-depend -
ent activator of secretion (CAPS) protein family, which 
is calcium binding proteins that regulate the exocytosis 
of synaptic and dense-core vesicles in neurons and neu -
roendocrine cells. CADPS2 dysfunction has been linked 
to several psychiatric diseases, including autism and 
schizophrenia [109, 110]. The 13q21.32 locus includes 
PCDH9 which encodes a member of the protocadherin 
family of transmembrane proteins containing cadherin

domains. These proteins mediate cell adhesion in neu -

be involved in signaling at neuronal synaptic junctions 
and has been implicated in depression and autism [111, 
112]. SNPs near PCDH9 have also been associated with 
depression, education, smoking behavior, insomnia, well-
being, and neuroticism in GWAS, and while these SNPs 
did not reach our threshold for significance (P < 5 ×  10−6), 
they were also associated with BRS (P < 0.05).
Reciprocal replication attempts of our BRS loci and 
those reported in two previous GWAS of self-assessed 
resilience (STARRS [9] and RS-11 [10]) were largely 
unsuccessful. The RS-11 and STARRS resilience scores 
have not been directly compared to BRS in a single 
cohort. The moderate genetic correlations we observed 
suggest different genetic architectures or constructs rep -
resented by these measures of resilience. Interestingly, 
the genetic correlations observed among measures of 
resilience were weaker than between BRS and neuroti -
cism which may reflect (i) different constructs captured 
by BRS, RS-11, and STARRS, and (ii) bias due to over -
lapping samples contributing to BRS and neuroticism 
results. Our sample size is eight times larger than these 
prior GWAS efforts and might have been expected to 
replicate loci if they were true positives and shared by the 
different measures of resilience. However, since resilience 
has many definitions and associated phenotypes, differ -
ences between GWAS studies are to be expected. Our 
gene-based analyses confirmed L3MBTL2, a proposed 
resilient gene [7] harboring variants linked to depres -
sion, anxiety, neuroticism, and well-being in GWAS, but 
its encoded protein has no known biological link to these 
traits.
The large sample size and the first application of 
GWAS to BRS are strengths of the current study. BRS has 
amongst the highest psychometric ratings of resilience 
measures and has been widely applied in the field across 
various populations [15]. Our factor analysis of BRS 
items inferred a single factor solution consistent with 
most other studies in healthy populations [14, 20, 113, 
114]. BRS is unique in that all items reflect a sense of per-
sonal agency [1]. As adaptation to adversity is inherently 
dynamic, many researchers view resilience as a process 
or as a trajectory. However, others have defined resilience 
as a stable trait [84, 115]. Measures of resilience slightly 
increase over time but remain correlated [51, 116–119]. 
Resilience of individuals near the tails of the population 
distribution is especially stable overtime [51]. An extreme 
trait GWAS of BRS we conducted yielded similar results 
(data not shown) thus supporting the notion of a gener -
ally stable trait. Limitations of our study should be con -
sidered when interpreting the findings. Results are based 
on a subsample of the UKB that completed a follow-up 
online questionnaire. This subsample is not representa -
tive of the full UKB or UK more generally [120, 121]. Ref-
erent resources for genetic correlations were also largely 
based on UKB. Independent replication and extension to 
other ancestries are warranted. Another limitation is that 
our resilience measure is a single questionnaire, the BRS, 
and does not capture the full range of resilience measures 
that are available in the literature.
Conclusions
In summary, our findings provide further support for 
a genetic basis to trait resilience and one shared with 
psychiatric disorders and personality traits. Whether 
findings of the current study support a causal role of 
resilience in these psychiatric traits, or a shared genetic 
liability, warrants further investigation. Some of our 
promising loci are novel and if replicated offer new bio -
logical insight to resilience and for the development of 
more pharmacologic interventions for multiple psychiat -
ric pathologies.
